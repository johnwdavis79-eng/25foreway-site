Chapter 1: The Mine

By John W. Davis. About 5,300 words, roughly 21 minutes.

Merkers, Thuringia — April 10–12, 1945

The Reichsbank, when the Third Army found it, was in a hole in the ground, which was the only place in Germany that had not been bombed, and the only place in Germany where nobody had yet thought to ask for a receipt.

Baldwin heard about it on the eleventh of April, in the new headquarters at Hersfeld. The Army had moved there from Frankfurt that morning, three hundred men and nine hundred filing cabinets arriving in the rain like a circus that had lost its elephants. Beyond the offices stood long military sheds. The G-4 section had found space near the gymnasium; General Muller's desk faced a painted wall map of the Reich, already pinned with every city that mattered and a few that did not.

“Merkers,” said Major Teague, and put a finger on the map.

Major Teague was new. He had come out from Plans in the last week of February to take the chair that had been Pike’s, and he sat in it the way a man sits in a rented tuxedo, upright, taking care of the creases. He was from Dayton, Ohio. He was thirty-four, round, tidy, pink, with a pencil behind each ear and a third in his hand, and he believed with his whole heart that the war could be won by a form properly completed in triplicate. In a general way, it was how the Army had done it. It was only that Pike had known it was a joke, and Teague did not, and in the section room at Hersfeld there was a faint sense, at all hours, of a man carrying a very full cup across a room with no floor.

“It’s a salt mine,” said Teague. “Twenty-some miles. XII Corps’ people got into it on the eighth. A lot of money’s in it.”

“How much money?”

“They don’t know yet. They’ve stopped counting at a certain point to start again.”

Baldwin looked at the map. The pin at Merkers was small and blue and very close to the others, and he felt for the first time the strangeness of the last three weeks, in which the Army had gone from the Rhine to the middle of Germany at a speed that should have been impossible, since for the first time since August it had had roads, and bridges, and a broken railway net the engineers were laboring to put back together, and German towns that came out to meet it with white bedsheets hung from every window like an enormous and rather reproachful laundry. He had been on the Third Army staff for fifteen months, and he had never, in the whole of that time, seen it so well supplied or so confused about what it was for.

“What do they need from G-4?”

“Trucks,” said Teague, and pushed the paper across.

It was a single sheet. It had come down from Twelfth Army Group by way of the Supreme Commander’s staff, and it said that a quantity of Reichsbank property had been found in the Kaiseroda mine at Merkers, that the property was to be removed to Frankfurt for safekeeping under the direction of the Finance Department, and that the Third Army would provide, on a date to be fixed, such motor transport and guards as might be required. The word quantity was the only number on the page.

“Ten-ton trucks,” said Teague, “is my guess. I’ve asked what they’d be carrying. I’ve been told it’s—” he consulted a second paper—“valuables.”

At his desk General Muller, who had not looked up, turned a page.

“Captain Baldwin,” said Muller.

“Sir.”

“Look.”

It was, so far as anyone in the section could later remember, the longest instruction he gave that day.

They went down to Merkers in the jeep, Lusk driving, Epstein in the back with a Thompson gun and a German phrase book that he used as a pillow, and Wray in the second seat with the map folded to the correct square and his finger on it, holding the country in place. The road was one of the good ones. The Germans had built it for the war. It ran through a country of low hills and villages and orchards just coming into blossom, and everywhere the same sheet hung out of every upper window, and in the doorways the old women stood with their arms folded and watched the Army go by with the weary civility of a ticket collector.

“They look like they’ve been expecting us,” said Lusk.

“They have been.”

“They look like we’re late.”

“We are a little late, I think,” said Epstein, from the back. “They have had the sheet out since Tuesday.”

Lusk was in good spirits. His shoulder, which had spent the winter in a sling, was a great deal better, and he could now hold the wheel with both hands and do a thing with the gearshift that he described as a pass at the Pope, and he had recently, for the first time since Okmulgee, obtained a pair of boots that fit.

The mine was in a village at the foot of a wooded rise, a little gray mining place with a church and a pub and slag heaps like the backs of sleeping elephants, and the pithead was a steel headframe, rust-brown, with a wheel on top, set in a yard full of Army. The yard was thick with it. Jeeps nosed in the mud, and trucks, and a German ambulance with a Red Cross and a field telephone wire running from it to a pole, and soldiers of the 90th Division in a state of watchful boredom, leaning on their rifles in the sun. Somebody had put up a hand-lettered sign that said KEEP OUT, in English, and under it, in German, a smaller one that said the same, and under that, in pencil, in a different hand, Please.

“Sir,” said Lusk. “I’d like it known.”

“What?”

“I ain’t going down.”

“Nobody has asked you to.”

“I know it, sir. I’m saying it early.” He set the brake and folded his hands on the wheel. “Back home we got a rule about holes. A man don’t go down one he didn’t dig, and he don’t go down one he can’t see the bottom of, and he don’t go down any that’s got a elevator. It’s the first thing they tell you. It’s right in front of the hymnal.”

“It is a mine, Corporal,” said Epstein. “You have been in a cellar.”

“A cellar’s a room, Sergeant. A cellar knows what it is.”

❦

The officer who received them at the pithead was a lieutenant colonel of the Finance Department, a short square tired man in a trench coat and a pair of steel spectacles, who looked at Baldwin’s captain’s bars and Baldwin’s orders and Baldwin, in that order, with the weary courtesy of forty hours awake. Most of the people coming through the door wanted something. He was a lawyer. He had been a lawyer before the war and a lawyer in the Treasury, and he spoke exactly, as though nothing were true until it was on a piece of paper in his own hand. He did not tell Baldwin what was in the mine. He said that he would show him, which was a different thing, and he took him across the yard to a small wooden hut and gave him a helmet with a lamp.

Baldwin put it on. It was too small.

“The cage,” said the Colonel, “is rated for twelve. We use it for six.”

It was a steel box with a mesh door, roughly the size of a freight elevator, and it hung from a cable at the lip of a black square in the earth from which came a faint steady warm wind that smelled of cellars and pennies. A German miner worked the lever, an elderly man in a leather apron who had apparently not been told that the war had changed hands and went on running the machine in his customary silence, with the air of an elevator operator of thirty years’ standing. The door shut. The floor dropped. And Baldwin, who had been up in an airplane exactly twice and down a well once, felt his stomach stay where it was while the rest of him departed.

It took, by his watch, fifty-five seconds. The Colonel told him afterward that it was a little over two thousand feet. The walls went by in the helmet’s lamp, gray and white and glittering, and at certain levels there were openings in them, black mouths with timber props, and from one a rail track went off into the dark with a tub on it; and the air got warm, and then warmer, and quite abruptly, as if a window had been opened, the cage stopped and the mesh door rolled back on a cathedral.

It was salt. It was all salt. The walls were salt, and the ceiling, and the floor of the gallery, which had been cut square and high as a hangar, and which went off in both directions out of sight under a line of electric bulbs on a drooping wire; and under the lights the whole of it glittered, in a dry pale pink-and-white shimmer, like the inside of a geode. Their boots made no sound. It was warm and dry and very still, and he had a sense, which he could not afterward place, that he had come in out of a storm into a very large and well-kept house, belonging to someone who had gone out and left every lamp burning.

They walked three-quarters of a mile.

At the end of the gallery was a brick wall with a hole blown in it. The edges of the hole were fresh. A steel door, very old and very thick, hung beside it on its hinges, with the key never found and the lock punched through. Two MPs of the 90th stood on either side of the hole with their rifles slung, and one of them, a boy with an unlit cigarette behind his ear, looked at Baldwin and the Colonel dully. He had been standing guard over something for thirty hours and had begun to find it funny.

“Evening, Colonel.”

“It is morning.”

“Not down here, sir.”

They went through.

The room was the size of a gymnasium. It had a high arched ceiling and a floor of pale salt, and the lights ran down the middle on a wire, and along both walls, and across the floor from one end to the other, in rows as neat as a seed catalog, knee-high, stood bags. They were canvas. They were stacked three and four deep, tied at the neck, each with a paper seal and a number in black. They went on to the back wall. There was a path down the middle, wide enough for one man, and on either side the bags lay in their ranks without the faintest stir, brown and solid and patient, like sheep who have been told to wait.

“Gold,” said the Colonel. “Bars. Some coin. The bags are sealed, and we are opening them in sequence. We have been opening them since the eighth.”

“How many?”

“We are counting. I will tell you what I tell everyone who comes down. I do not want to hear anybody say a figure. I have been given four, and each was said with perfect confidence, and each was different. When the count is done it will be written down. When it is written down I will say it. Until then I would be obliged if you would think of it as a good deal.”

“Yes, sir.”

“It is, in fact, I am given to understand, the entire gold reserve of the German Reich.” He said it flatly, a number in a timetable. “Which was supposed to be in Berlin. Somebody thought it would be better in a salt mine. That was either the shrewdest decision the Reichsbank made in the war, or the stupidest, and I’m not able to say which until I know who they thought would get here first.”

Baldwin stood in the path. Beside his boot lay a bag with its seal broken, and the neck turned back, and in it, close-packed in the lamp, like loaves in a baker’s tray, pale yellow, dull, with a stamp on each, a row of small bars. He bent and touched one. It was cold. It was heavier than a thing that size had any right to be; he had put two fingers under the end of it, to lift, as he might have lifted a brick, and it stayed on the floor of the bag with the calm indifference of a mountain, and he took his hand back and looked at it.

He did arithmetic. He couldn’t help it. It was how his mind went into a room.

A hundred million dollars was a figure, and he could not feel a figure; he could feel a ton. There were, by the look of the nearer rows, some tons of this stuff, a good many tons, and it would have to go up the shaft in a cage rated for twelve and used for six, and across the yard to a truck, and a hundred miles along the road to Frankfurt, over every bridge between; and he thought, with a clean prickle of usefulness, The axles.

“Colonel,” he said. “What does a truck have to carry?”

The Colonel looked at him.

“It’s the weight,” Baldwin said. “I can give you ten-ton trucks. Ten tons is a rating for a load that’s spread. This is dense. If we fill a body with it, we break the spring or the axle or the bridge, and then it’s on the road, in a ditch, with a hole in the side.”

“I had not thought of that.”

“I wouldn’t expect you to. It’s not a lawyer’s problem.”

“It is, now. Captain. Could you tell me what you need to know?”

“Sir,” said Baldwin, “I need to know what it weighs.”

“We’re counting.”

“That’s the trouble, sir. So am I.”

The receipt was Teague’s idea.

It had been developing in him since morning, with the slow unstoppable force of an idea whose time has come, and on the afternoon of the eleventh, when Baldwin came back up into the daylight with his ears popping and his uniform dusted pink to the knees, he found it waiting for him in the yard with a typewriter.

“Captain,” said Teague. He had driven over himself, in the section’s second jeep, with a clerk and a typewriter and an attaché case, and he stood in the mud beside the pithead with his pencils in his ears. “I’ve been thinking about the dispatch form.”

“What about it?”

“It has to be filled. It’s the regulation. No supplies move on a Third Army vehicle without a receipt from the shipper to the carrier. We take custody at the pithead, we sign for the load, and we turn it over at the other end to the consignee, who signs. Otherwise nobody’s responsible.”

“Teague, it is the German gold reserve.”

“That’s why, Captain.”

It was, Baldwin conceded afterward, a thing he could not fault. The form was a long oblong sheet, buff, and in the file it was the humblest document in the Army, the sort on which a three-quarter-ton truck went from a motor pool in Verdun to a depot in Toul with a load of spark plugs; and it had four columns, and a line at the bottom for the signature of the shipper, and a stamp. He took it from Teague. It had been filled in already, in Teague’s small neat hand, as far as it could be: the date, the place, the vehicle numbers, to be assigned; the carrier, Third Army, G-4; the consignee, Finance Department, Frankfurt. Under Cargo the column was ruled in five boxes, and the first box said Class of Supply.

“There it is,” said Teague.

There they were. The five classes, in the Army’s old and honorable order. Class I was rations, and forage, and the stuff that men and animals put in their mouths. Class II was clothing and tools, Class III was gasoline and oil, Class IV was, in their daily business, engineer stock, lumber and wire and machinery, and Class V was ammunition. Beside each, Teague had written a figure, and under each a short line ruled for the number of tons, and Baldwin saw, with a sudden sinking sensation which he had last felt in a vault, that every one of the lines was empty.

“The clerk wants to know,” said Teague, “which it is.”

The clerk was a technician fourth grade, a very young man from Scranton named Loomis, with a long neck and freckles and a pair of glasses mended with adhesive tape, and he sat at a folding table in the yard with the typewriter in front of him and his hands raised above the keys, in the posture of a pianist who has been asked for a piece nobody has written.

“Gold’s not rations, sir,” said Loomis.

“No.”

“It’s not clothing.”

“I would say not.”

“It’s not POL, sir. Unless you melt it.”

“Loomis, nobody is going to melt it.”

“No, sir. I’m only saying, for the form.” He turned a page of his manual with great care. “It’s not ammunition. I thought that for a minute. On account of what it was supposed to be for. But I don’t think it’s Class V, sir. I think you’d have to show it was used.”

“It’s Class IV,” said Teague.

“Sir, Class IV is construction. Lumber. Wire. Cement.”

“It’s a kind of material.”

“It’s not for building, sir.”

“It is possible,” said Teague, “that it will be used in building something.”

“What?”

“I don’t know, Loomis.”

There was a silence. In the pale spring sun, in the yard, among the jeeps, the MPs of the 90th stood very still. One of them was biting the inside of his cheek. The old German miner at the cage lever had turned his back, and his shoulders, in the leather apron, were moving in a way that suggested he had understood more English than he had previously let on.

Lusk, who had been standing beside the jeep with his hat pushed back and his hands in his belt in a state of complete spiritual repose, said, “Sir, if I may.”

“No,” said Baldwin.

“Put it down as Class Five, sir. Ammunition. It’s what they were shooting with.”

Loomis took his hands off the keys. He looked at Lusk. He looked at Baldwin. He looked, last, at the manual, and Baldwin saw a thing come into his face which was not quite hope, and was not quite its opposite, but belonged to the family: a very junior man discovering that the book has a page for everything but this, and beginning, with the pale first stirrings of independence, to enjoy it.

“Sir,” said Loomis. “There’s Class Four.”

“There is?”

“Here, sir.” He kept his finger on the place. “Supplies with no prescribed allowance. If we write miscellaneous after it, sir. It's where the Army puts the things it can't, it's—”

“Loomis.”

“Yes, sir?”

“Type it.”

He typed it. It took him four seconds. He pulled the sheet out of the machine with a ratcheting zip and held it up in the sun, and there it was, in the first box, in clean black capitals, the entire treasure of the Third Reich: CLASS IV, MISCELLANEOUS.

❦

It was Epstein who got the German clerk’s signature, and that was the part of the business Baldwin kept, afterward, when the rest had thinned.

They had found him in the pub. He was the last custodian, the Reichsbank’s own man, a stooped bald patient official of perhaps sixty in a black coat and a high collar, who had been sent down with the gold from Berlin in February and had stayed with it, evidently, because nobody had told him to stop. He sat at a table in the corner of the taproom under a stuffed boar’s head with his hat on the table and a small brown cardboard folder in front of him and his hands crossed on the folder. He had been interrogated by four officers, one of whom had spoken some German. He rose when Baldwin came in. He bowed, from the waist, a short formal old-fashioned bow, and said a sentence of some length, and Epstein, at Baldwin’s shoulder, said, “He says he is at our disposal, and that the books are in order.”

“Thank him.”

Epstein did. The clerk bowed again. He opened the cardboard folder, took out a sheet of paper, and set it on the table before Baldwin, squared to the edge, with a gesture so exact and so formal that Baldwin looked at it before he could stop himself. It was a receipt.

It was typed. It was on the Reichsbank’s own paper, with the eagle at the head, and it was dated the twenty-second of March, 1945, in the town of Merkers, and it recorded the receipt by the undersigned of a quantity of sealed bags, numbered, described by weight, in a column of figures that ran down the page for the length of the page; and at the foot, below a dotted line, there was a space for the signature of the person to whom custody was being transferred.

“He asks,” said Epstein, “to whom he should make it out.”

“To the United States Army.”

Epstein said it. The clerk listened. He seemed to consider. He said something, quietly, and then looked at his hands.

“He says it can’t be the United States Army, sir. He says that is a body, and the form requires a person. He says that is the whole point of the form.”

“Then to me.”

“He says you are only the carrier.”

“Then to the colonel from Finance.”

Epstein conveyed it. The clerk shook his head. He spoke at greater length, and with a kind of mild distress, as if he had been asked to explain a rule of grammar to a child, and Epstein listened to the end with his head on one side.

“He says it cannot be made out to the colonel, because the colonel is not the Reichsbank’s successor. He says that, for him, the Reichsbank has ceased to exist. He says he has been trying for six days to think of a person to whom he is responsible, and he has not found one.” Epstein cleared his throat. “He says that it is, with respect, a new situation.”

Baldwin looked at the old man. The old man looked at the table. Over his head the boar’s glass eye gleamed in the lamplight with an expression of sustained malevolence, and in the taproom’s silence a clock ticked, a small tinny one, a cuckoo’s, that struck, at that moment, the half hour, and produced its bird, which said its piece with the hesitation of a thing that had not been wound in a year.

“Tell him,” said Baldwin, “to make it out to the next one.”

“The next what, sir?”

“Whoever it is. Whoever comes after. Tell him to leave the line blank, and sign at the bottom, and I will write the name in when I know it.”

Epstein said it. He said it in German, in his careful Brooklyn German, which was, as Kranz had once remarked, almost Frankfurt, and the clerk heard it out, and a curious thing happened to his face. It did not soften. It simply stopped being strained. A column that had not footed all morning had been shown the line that carries the balance forward.

He signed. He took a steel pen from his coat, and wiped it, and wrote Reinhard, with the surname after it, in a hand that did not shake. He blotted it. He gave it to Baldwin with both hands, and bowed, and said one word.

“He says, thank you,” said Epstein. “He says it is the first time in some weeks that he has been told what to do.”

It was in the afternoon of that day, with the receipt in his briefcase and the packing arrangements beginning, that Baldwin went down a second time, to inspect the stores, and found that the thing he had come to count was not the only thing in the room.

He did not go in at once. At the back of the vault there was a second door, a smaller one, of wood, and the MP at it, a corporal from the 90th with an old face and a Missouri voice, said, “You’ll want to look at this one, Captain, and then you’ll want to decide whether you want to have looked.” He opened it.

It was a storeroom. It was low, and dry, and lit by a single bulb. There were shelves. On the shelves, and on the floor in front of the shelves, and in piles against the wall, were suitcases. Some were leather. Some were cardboard, made to look like leather. They were of all sizes, and they were scuffed, and some of them still had the paper labels tied to the handles with string, and on the labels, in pencil, in a number of hands, were names. Beside the suitcases stood a number of canvas bags, of a smaller size than the bags of gold, tied at the neck. A line of them were open. A man from Finance in a green eyeshade was working through the nearest, with a ledger and a pair of tweezers and a face of absolute professional blankness, and he looked up as Baldwin came to the threshold, and then down.

On the table in front of him in a row were laid out, on a white cloth, what he had taken from the bag. Baldwin looked at them for what he judged afterward to have been a good deal longer than was necessary. They were small. They were yellow. Some were still set in broken dental work. There were rings among them, plain gold wedding rings, a great many, tumbled together like beads, and spectacle frames, and a gold watch with a worn strap, and a child’s silver spoon.

He did not understand it, and then he did.

The man from Finance did not speak. He turned the page of the ledger. In the margin of the page, in his tiny upright hand, he had written, in one column, a number, and in the next, under the heading Description, the words dental gold, assorted. He wrote them as he would have written lumber. There was nothing else he could write. The form, as Baldwin knew, had no other word.

The corporal at the door cleared his throat.

“They were in a bag that said Melmer, sir,” he said. “That’s the name on the label. The Colonel says it’s a man in the SS. He says they sent it all to the bank to be weighed and put in with the rest.”

“With the rest.”

“Yes, sir. Same vault. A separate account.” The corporal looked at the floor. “He says they kept very good books.”

Baldwin stood in the doorway. The bulb hummed. Behind him, in the long salt gallery, the cage rattled down, and a voice shouted in German, and a boot rang on a plank, and he heard Loomis, who had been permitted to come down because he was the only man who could work the typewriter in a mine, say in a high voice to someone he could not see, “Sir, I need a line for this.”

He turned. Loomis stood in the gallery, with the dispatch form held in front of him in both hands, white to the lips, with the freckles standing out on his face like spilled pepper, and he was not looking at the table. He was looking at Baldwin.

“Sir. Which class?”

There was a pause.

“None,” said Baldwin.

“I can’t leave it blank, sir. They’ll send it back.”

“Then write it in the remarks.”

“What do I write?”

Baldwin took the form. He took the pencil from behind Teague’s ear, which Teague had lent him with a look of having been touched by something he could not name, and he held the form against the wall and wrote, in the space at the bottom headed Remarks, in his own small upright script, the only line on the page that was not typed.

Property of persons unknown. To be held for return. Do not melt.

He gave it back. Loomis read it. He read it again, and he did not say a word, and he went away down the gallery with it, and Baldwin heard the keys begin to chatter in the distance, one at a time, very carefully, as a person walks across a floor that he is not sure will hold.

The generals came on the morning of the twelfth.

They had flown from Hersfeld and reached the mine about half past ten; the 90th Division, which had been told to look sharp, looked sharp. Baldwin watched from the slag heap with Lusk and a thermos. His transport detail had assembled eleven vehicles, given each driver a numbered card and sent most of them ahead to Gotha before the party reached the mine. The generals would go on to XII Corps for lunch, then fly. The cars would already be at the landing ground.

They went down. The lift passed out of hearing. When they came up again, and stood in the yard, in a group, in the sun, three of them tall and one short, and several younger men with maps, and the Finance colonel in his trench coat who walked a pace behind them, and who looked, from a hundred yards, like a bookkeeper at a funeral. Nobody took off a hat. Baldwin could not hear what was said. He watched the faces. They had gone down laughing, he thought, or something near it, as men laugh who are about to be shown a very large and ridiculous sum; and they came up, and their faces had changed.

He did not know the exact moment it had happened. He knew only that the youngest of the three tall men, who was bareheaded and gray at the temples, said something short and low to the Finance colonel, and that the colonel nodded, once, and made a note.

“What do you suppose they saw, sir?” said Lusk.

“Everything.”

“I thought it was only the gold.”

“So did I,” said Baldwin.

He poured the last of the coffee. The headframe wheel turned slowly against the sky, with a click, and a gust brought up from the shaft the faint cellar smell of salt and pennies, and for a moment, on the slag heap above the village, the two of them watched the cars being turned in the yard, one at a time, in order of precedence, with their little flags.

“Sir. How was it? Down there.”

“Heavy.”

“Just heavy?”

“I will tell you when I know.”

“We'd better go, sir,” said Lusk. “The cars are at Gotha. The party flies in after lunch. Then there's a camp.”

“Which?”

“Ohrdruf. About thirty miles east of here. We got there last Wednesday, sir, the Fourth Armored did. The word’s been going round the motor pool.” He stopped. He looked at the cars. For once in his life Lusk had nothing to add, and he sat with his thermos cup in both hands and his hat pushed back and his mouth a little open, and said, after a time, in the plain flat voice he used for the truth, “I never heard a thing, sir, that made me want so much to be wrong about it.”
