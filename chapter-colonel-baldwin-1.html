Chapter 1: The Lobby

By John W. Davis. About 3,400 words, roughly 14 minutes.

Enniskillen, County Fermanagh, Northern Ireland — Friday, May 12, 1944

Friday luncheon at the Lough Erne Arms had run to gray mutton, potatoes that had given up, and suet pudding under pale custard. Capt. Lewis W. Baldwin Jr. of the Third United States Army had eaten all of it, praised the mustard to the waitress, and carried a cup of parsnip-flavored coffee to the armchair beside the lobby grate. Over the reception desk the clock stood at five minutes past one. That left an hour and five minutes to deliver himself, two military policemen, a driver from Hamtramck, Michigan, and a brown leather briefcase to the 2:10 for Belfast and the Larne boat. He had never missed a train in his life.

The briefcase stood between his boots. Until Wednesday a steel chain had linked its handle to a cuff on his left wrist, turning every bowl of hotel soup into minor theater. Coming out of an 8th Division Nissen hut at Omagh in the rain, the chain snagged the iron latch and parted with a polite tink. The division sergeant stared at the loose links—“a hell of a thing, sir, I never seen one do that”—and offered twine. Baldwin pocketed the chain and skipped the twine. Scuffed at the corners, dark at the grip, the case looked like something a country solicitor carried into court or a cattle buyer to a fair. Inside, in three manila folders, lay the corrected movement and loading tables for the follow-on formations in Northern Ireland: serials, ports, dates, ships, and the stowage order for guns, trucks, and gasoline going into the holds. Each folder bore the red stamp TOP SECRET and, beneath it in purple capitals of incongruous cheer, ORANGE.

Somebody at Peover Hall had picked that word out of a codebook without ever setting foot in County Fermanagh, where the Orange lodges marched every July behind fifes and drums and banners of King William on a white horse. In Enniskillen they might as well have stamped the folders PROTESTANT and hired a flute band.

Still, the chair was deep, and after three days of tin huts the lobby suited him. He knew this breed of hotel from boyhood trips down the Missouri Pacific with his father, give or take an ocean: dark wood cured in wet wool and whiskey, brass rods polished every dawn out of moral duty, a carpet faded from red to dried blood between street door and bar, and a long window framing a gray street that looked pasted in an album. Turf burned in the grate and smelled like a farm in heaven. Across the hearth, two RAF officers from the flying-boat base up the lough at Castle Archdale had split the Impartial Reporter down its fold and were conducting a low, murderous English quarrel over a dog. Near the stairs a cattle dealer in a wet tweed cap leaned over a thin man in a long black coat, building toward the turn of a story; every minute or two the dealer threw his head back and let loose a wet countryman’s laugh that boomed up the stairwell and rolled back down.

The man in the long black coat never stirred. Spine straight, knees together, he sat by the window holding a newspaper of his own. From Baldwin’s chair it appeared to be upside down.

Baldwin drew on his cigarette and studied the sheet through the smoke. Either the Germans had sent Fermanagh an agent who could not tell top from bottom on newsprint, or an old farmer had come to town without his spectacles and refused to give the room the satisfaction of knowing it. Baldwin put two shillings on pride. Outside a girl in a mackintosh stopped her bicycle to put a loaf in the basket. Something in the impatient way she did it made him think of Anne getting ready for a party, with the car waiting and one glove still lost. For a moment he was in St. Louis. Then a brewer’s dray passed the window.

“Sir.”

Sgt. Stanley Dombrowski leaned one forearm on the back of the chair. He was broad-shouldered and soft-voiced, with an open face, a boxer’s thickened ear, and a gift for saying filthy things in a voice that belonged in a parish parlor.

“The MPs are asking about tea again, sir. They say they’re perishing. Lusk learned it Wednesday night off a girl in Omagh, and now he puts it on everything like catsup. Been perishing since breakfast.”

“Tell Lusk he may perish over a cup in the car.”

“Told him that, sir. He says in an automobile it ruins the stomach. What he said was tea’s like a woman, you gotta sit down at a table and take your hat off for it.”

“Did he.”

“I’m cleaning it up, sir.”

“I know you are, Sergeant. Clean it much further and there won’t be a verb left.”

Dombrowski’s mouth twitched. “I’ll bring the car round,” he said, and went out to the curb, where a black Humber staff car on loan from the British waited with two American military policemen wedged in the back and the air of a car parked by men ready to leave the county.

Over the desk the clock clicked to seventeen past one. Baldwin had asked reception to put a trunk call through to Base Section movements in Belfast. Nobody in Enniskillen spoke of the 2:10 in the indicative mood; the station porter had discussed it that morning the way Baldwin’s mother discussed country cousins, as something rumored to be coming if nothing livelier intervened. Before any trip with his wife, Baldwin read timetables backward from harbor pier to suburban platform while she watched him over a martini and asked whether he meant to fire the boiler himself. He wanted Belfast to say the Larne boat would hold. He liked to see the whole board.

His father had said once at dinner that a railroad was nothing but a promise that a thing would be somewhere at a certain time, that any fool could make the promise and only a few men alive could keep it, and had gone back to his soup. He gave you one sentence like that a year, and you lived on it.

“Captain Baldwin?”

Behind the counter Mrs. Corrigan lifted her glasses on their chain and set them on her nose. She had given her name when he signed the book and nothing beyond it, a reserve he respected; she was sixty or near it, a widow by the worn gold band on a hand that did all its own scrubbing, and whenever she addressed a guest she put the glasses on first, auditing him over the rims like a doubtful check.

“Your call’s through. The box is down the passage, on the left. You’ll want to shout. The line from Belfast does be very poor in the afternoons.”

“Thank you, Mrs. Corrigan.”

Baldwin stood. Down the dark passage waited the telephone box—mahogany and frosted glass, narrow as an upended coffin, its door rigged on a fierce spring and its tiny ledge crowded by a directory. Wedging six feet of captain, a burning cigarette, a notebook, and a fat briefcase inside it while shouting to Belfast was a geometry problem with no neat answer. Six yards off, Mrs. Corrigan had bent back over her register; by the grate the Castle Archdale fliers were still deep in their dog. Baldwin slid the case against the front leg of his chair, tucked well into the shadow of its skirt where you would have to go looking to see it, and went down the passage with his cigarette in his mouth and his green notebook in his hand.

The line from Belfast sounded like a man frying bacon on the moon.

“Baldwin!” he shouted. “Third Army! Calling about the two-ten from Enniskillen and the Larne boat tonight!”

Through the crackle came an English voice, distant and cheerfully unbothered. “The what?”

“The two-ten!”

“Oh, that’s off.”

Baldwin took the cigarette from his mouth. “Off?”

“Cancelled since noon. Didn’t anybody tell you? There’s a fault on the line beyond Dungannon—something to do with a bridge, or a cow, or a cow on a bridge, nobody’s sure. If you want the boat you’ll have to drive. Leave now and don’t stop for anything and you’ll make her. Boat goes at seven. Shall I ring Larne and tell them a Yank’s coming in a hurry?”

“Please. Tell them a Yank is coming in a hurry.”

“Righto. Good luck with the cows.”

The line clicked dead. Baldwin backed through the spring door with his elbow. One-twenty. Five hours and forty minutes to the gangway. Ninety-odd miles in a strange Humber. He began on what he called the ladder, his father’s habit of taking a problem one question at a time: which road cleared Dungannon fastest, what pace Dombrowski could hold between stone ditches, where a sensible man went slow and a lucky one kept his foot down. Coming up the passage fast, he laid coins on the blotter. “Thank you, Mrs. Corrigan, we have to run.” She said, “God bless,” and he went out through the street door into the gray light. Behind him it closed on its brass hinge. The turf fire went on burning. The briefcase stood in the shadow of the chair skirt, exactly where he had put it.

“Driving,” Baldwin said, getting into the front seat. “The train’s off. Belfast says leave now and don’t stop for anything.”

“Larne?” Dombrowski asked, already pulling out.

“Larne. Seven o’clock boat.”

From the back came a stricken sigh. “Christ on a crutch,” said Cpl. Floyd Lusk. Lusk came from Okmulgee, Oklahoma, and talked in a slow, aggrieved drawl without stopping, as though issued all the words in Oklahoma and held accountable on a receipt. “No tea. Back home, Captain, county sheriff gives a bank robber sweet coffee before he rides him down to the penitentiary.”

“You’ll have tea in Larne.”

“Larne’s clear across the island, sir. Man’s throat dries up like harness leather in ninety miles.”

Beside him PFC Eddie Szabo—from Hamtramck, three streets off Dombrowski’s mother’s bar, built like a coal stove and stingy past forty words a day—shifted his carbine to give Lusk’s elbows room.

Dombrowski drove an unfamiliar car the way he played stud poker: quiet hands, level eyes, and a calm faith that narrow gaps widened for men who kept their nerve. Ordinarily he sang under his breath at the wheel, mangling the “Beer Barrel Polka” in two languages; he had sung it all the way from Omagh that morning. Now, measuring British steering and damp tar, he stayed quiet. The Humber swept past the barracks, the Orange hall, and a pub with a bicycle chained outside, rumbled over the bridge above the fast brown River Erne, and climbed into the hills.

White hawthorn foamed high on both banks, fencing pastures the green of a billiard table. Here and there a farmhouse stood alone under a curl of turf smoke; a farm dog raced the tires, judged the pace, and gave up; a woman pedaling a bicycle with a milk can in the basket waved as they passed. Lusk twisted round, grinning, and went on waving through the rear glass until Szabo, without looking round, laid one huge hand on his sleeve and lowered his arm into his lap.

Baldwin lit a fresh cigarette off the first and cracked his window an inch.

“You want the window, sir?” Dombrowski asked.

“No, thank you, Sergeant.”

“Lusk’s gonna want it. Minute his mouth stops moving in a closed car, he gets carsick.”

“Then we’re safe till Scotland,” Baldwin said.

Lusk laughed. Without turning his square head, Szabo said, “Still talking.”

“You leave my mama in Okmulgee—”

“Fellas,” Dombrowski murmured, gentle as a curate, and took a blind crest in third gear.

Thirty-five minutes went behind them before Baldwin noticed he had been comfortable for thirty-five minutes. With the glass cracked an inch, the notebook closed on his knee, and smoke curling from his right hand, he had stretched both legs out. His left hand lay open between his thigh and the door. Idly—the way you register a clock stopped in another room—it struck him that his left hand felt light. All week those fingers had carried the pull of a handle.

He looked down.

Black rubber floor.

His left boot—brown British leather bought in Knutsford, scuffed across the toe from the Nissen hut door at Omagh.

His right boot.

His hand dipped under the seat, touching grit, a map of County Tyrone, and one of Szabo’s chewing-gum wrappers. Nothing leather. Keeping his shoulders square, Baldwin looked into the back, where two military policemen sat with upturned helmets on their knees. Between their boots lay empty gray carpet.

Lusk’s mouth shut mid-word. “Sir?”

Baldwin checked his watch. Four minutes to two. Thirty-six minutes on the Belfast road. The numbers dropped through him: thirty-six out, thirty back if Dombrowski shaved every ditch to the river. An hour. Sixty minutes with three folders alone in a hotel lobby a few miles from a border smugglers crossed with a churn of butter on each shoulder, and south of that border sat Dublin. Every face from the parlor snapped back—the upside-down newspaper, the long black coat, the cattle dealer whose laugh echoed on the stairs, the two RAF officers, anyone in off the street since twenty past one. Ten minutes with a small camera took three folders. Five minutes on a bicycle cleared the town.

“Sergeant,” Baldwin said. “Turn the car around.”

Dombrowski did not ask. Dombrowski checked his mirror, glanced past a small boy with a donkey cart, braked at a farm gate, and put the Humber through a three-point turn so neat the donkey never raised its head.

“How fast, sir?”

“As fast as it goes.”

“Yes, sir.”

Tires bit gravel, caught the tar, and the white hedges blurred into walls. Neither MP asked what they were going back for. On Wednesday at Omagh, a major with a mustache had told them that if anything happened to the captain’s briefcase they would spend the war breaking rock in a stockade in a part of Scotland where the sun came out solely on the Fourth of July, and solely for the British. They had laughed outside the hut. In the mirror Lusk’s knuckles showed white on his helmet rim, and Szabo sat bolt upright.

Baldwin could not climb the ladder. He tried—what would an agent need, how would he know, where would he go—and each time the rungs slid down the wall. At the bottom waited one picture: the faded carpet, the shadow of the chair skirt, and a hand coming down to the handle. Whose hand, he could not see.

His cigarette burned to his fingers. He pinched the dead end out, held it in his fist, and watched the needle tremble past sixty.

They came back into Enniskillen at nineteen past two by the Town Hall clock, over the bridge and the brown river, past the pub with the chained bicycle and the Orange hall. Dombrowski brought the Humber to the curb so hard it rocked on its springs, and Baldwin was out before it stopped and through the street door.

The turf had sunk to red ash. The RAF officers had gone. The cattle dealer had gone. By the window the straight chair stood empty, the newspaper folded on its seat—upside down or right side up, nobody could tell.

Across the hearth stood Baldwin’s chair. Around its leg lay faded carpet. Under the skirt, nothing.

Baldwin stood in the middle of the rug, heart hammering in his throat. Behind him the street door sighed shut on its brass hinge; Lusk and Szabo halted inside with Dombrowski behind them. Everything gleamed with morning polish and moral duty, and Baldwin’s stomach pitched between being sick on the carpet and laughing aloud, either of which would finish him.

“Captain Baldwin,” said Mrs. Corrigan.

Behind the desk she lifted her glasses on their chain, settled them on her nose, and looked at him over the rims.

“You were in a terrible hurry.”

“Mrs. Corrigan.” His voice sounded thin and far off, like Belfast in the afternoon. “I left—”

“You left your case.” She bent behind the counter. A cupboard door clicked open. She came up with the brown leather briefcase in both hands and set it down on the blotter, square, as she set down the register. “I keep it under, when I see a thing left. Here, things that sit alone, Captain, they walk. You’d be surprised what walks in this town. Umbrellas. Bicycles. A whole ham walked out of here at Christmas and we never saw it again.”

He looked at the scuffed leather. He did not touch it.

There was a cigarette in his hand. He did not remember lighting it. He must have shaken one out of the pack and struck the match somewhere between the curb and the lobby, because there it was, burning, half an inch gone. He looked at it, and at the case. And here was the strange thing: he did not open the case. He stood at the desk of the Lough Erne Arms with the most important papers he would ever be trusted with a foot from his hand, two MPs and a sergeant from Hamtramck watching from the doorway and a widow with her glasses on a chain waiting, and he finished the cigarette.

He smoked it all the way down, slowly, like a man who had done it before. If you’re gonna smoke, look like you’ve done it before. He knew that if he did anything with his hands but smoke they would shake, and if they shook in front of Lusk and Szabo and Dombrowski he would never again be quite the same thing to them. So he smoked. He looked out the long window at the gray street. He tapped the ash into the brass tray on the desk. When it was done he put it out with three turns of the wrist, the way his father put out cigars.

Then he opened the case.

Three manila folders. TOP SECRET. ORANGE. He turned the sheets page by page while Mrs. Corrigan went back to her register. Every page was there, in order. Every clip sat where he had left it in Omagh, including the bent one on the second folder. A man could photograph a page and put the clip back. Baldwin went through the folders once more.

He snapped the clasp shut and laid his palm flat on the lid.

“Is it all there, then?” Mrs. Corrigan asked, eyes on her register.

“It’s all there.”

“That’s grand.”

“Mrs. Corrigan.” His hand moved toward his wallet—and stopped at the warning set of her mouth before she looked up. Lay money on that blotter and he would insult Mrs. Corrigan for life, along with the late Mr. Corrigan and half the town. He let his hand fall. “The gentleman by the window. In the long coat. Did he—”

“Mr. Maguire?” She looked up over the glasses. “Ah, he’s away to the fair at Lisnaskea. Cattle. He’s here every second Friday, God help us. Reads the paper the wrong way up to annoy his brother-in-law, that’s the man was with him. They haven’t spoken right since nineteen thirty-one.”

“I see.”

“You do not,” she said, kindly. “But it doesn’t matter.”

He picked up the case. His left arm swore it weighed five pounds more than at one o’clock, though he knew what paper weighed.

“Thank you,” he said. It came out too small. He tried again. “Thank you, Mrs. Corrigan. I won’t forget it.”

“You will,” she said. “Everyone does. That’s all right.” She took off her glasses and let them fall on their chain, and for the first time she looked at him straight, with no glass between them, and he saw that she knew exactly what she had kept under her desk for an hour, or knew enough, or knew the shape of it the way you know the shape of a thing in the dark, and had decided to know no more. “Now go on with you, Captain,” she said. “Go fast.”
