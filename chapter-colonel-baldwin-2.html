Chapter 1: Thirty-One Trains

By John W. Davis. About 3,900 words, roughly 16 minutes.

Le Mans — August 17, 1944

“How much,” said Muller, “where, and by when.”

It was a quarter past three in the afternoon, and Baldwin had not been to bed, and the field telephone in the dispatcher’s tower smelled of the dispatcher’s coffee and of the dispatcher. Below him in the yard the thirty-first gasoline train since midnight stood at the long platform with its engine sighing, and three hundred men were taking cans off it by hand and passing them down a line to the trucks, the way his grandmother’s church in Kentucky had passed buckets at a barn fire in 1911, a story he had heard so often as a boy that he could have drawn the barn, and the cow, and the deacon who had dropped the first bucket.

“Seven hundred and some thousand gallons in the yard, sir, give or take the last two trains.” He looked at the train sheet, which was the dispatcher’s, and which the dispatcher would not let out of his sight. “Twenty-one thousand went up to the XV Corps dump at Sées earlier in the week. Twelve thousand have been cleared for use; the rest is held for testing. The rest of the siding is still at Les Aulnaies, twenty-two wagons, waiting on an engine. Marchais says this afternoon.”

“Marchais says.”

“He said it in French, sir. It sounded more definite.”

A pause on the line, which in Muller was the same as a laugh. Behind it Baldwin could hear the G-4 tent: typewriters, a telephone, Pike saying something about Chartres to somebody who was clearly not enjoying it.

“Where,” said Muller.

“Wherever the general is going, sir. Which I would like very much to know.”

“So would the general. Come back. Bring numbers.” And he rang off.

The dispatcher took his train sheet back, gently, and smoothed it with both hands. He was a T/Sgt. from Scranton named Oberholtzer, and he had spent fifteen hours learning that the captain with the gold watch was a man who could be trusted near paper.

“You get used to him?” he said. “Your colonel?”

“No,” said Baldwin. “That’s the point of him.”

❦

Lucky Forward that week was a field near Le Mans, which is to say a field near a great many other fields: apple trees, tents dug in to the shoulder, trailers pulled under camouflage nets, and in the middle a map tent where the general’s war was drawn each morning in grease pencil on acetate and rubbed out each night with a rag that some unfortunate corporal guarded like the Host.

Baldwin spread the map flat and stood back from it.

The arrows went east. All of them. Fat blue arrows, the pencil laid on so thick it shone, fanning out of the Le Mans country in three directions at once as if someone had flung a handful of blue paint at the board and the paint had decided to keep going: one at Dreux, one at Chartres, one at Orléans on the Loire. Beyond them there was nothing at all, only the clean map, the towns in their small black print, the roads like veins on the back of a hand, all the way to the Seine, to Paris, and if you let your eye run, to the Meuse and the Moselle and the Rhine, where the map ended at the edge of the board in a strip of brown tape.

“Don’t,” said Pike, beside him.

“Don’t what?”

“Look at it like that. You look at it like that and you’ll start to believe it.” Pike was eating a C-ration biscuit with an expression of deep personal grievance and smoking a cigar at the same time, a feat Baldwin had never seen another man attempt. The unlit one, the Palermo one, rode in his breast pocket where it always did, the band showing over the flap like a medal. “The general looks at it like that. The general is paid to. You and me are paid to look at this.” He turned his back on the map and pointed the cigar across the tent at the other board.

The G-4 board had no arrows. It had columns: the classes of supply down the left side in Roman numerals, the days across the top, figures in the boxes in four different hands, crossed out and written in and crossed out. And at the bottom, in red, in Muller’s hand, one entry that did not change from day to day, because it was not a figure about the war. It was a figure about France.

Railhead: LE MANS.

“Dreux is ninety miles from that railhead,” said Pike. “Chartres about the same. Orléans more. And the general does not intend to stop at any of the three.”

“Every mile east is a mile farther from the gas.”

“Every mile east is two miles, Texas, because the trucks have to come back.”

Pike had called him Texas since Fort Sam Houston, where the Army had kept him two years and he had never once worn the hat; a St. Louis man answers to Texas the way a cat answers to Rover, which is to say when it suits him. Baldwin knew the arithmetic. He had known it on the platform in the dark with the trains coming in, the whole long beautiful night of them, and had not let himself think it, because it was a night for being happy and he had not had many. He took out the notebook with the names in it, turned past the names without looking, and did the sum again at the back in pencil, the way his father had taught him at the kitchen table at Westmoreland Place, with the carrying figures written small above the line.

It came out the same.

“Huh,” he said.

“Yeah,” said Pike. “Huh.” He laid the biscuit on the edge of the map table as if setting down a wreath. “You want the worst of it? It’s working. All of it. We have the finest army in the history of the world doing the finest thing an army ever did, and it’s going so fast it’s running away from its own lunch.”

“So we make the lunch faster.”

“I have been in this Army since Coolidge,” said Pike, “and I have never once seen anybody make lunch faster. You can make it bigger. You can make it worse. You can make it closer, if you’ve got the trucks.” He picked up the biscuit again and regarded it with loathing. “We have not got the trucks.”

❦

Halloran came over from the G-2 tent that evening with a folder under his arm and his spectacles pushed up on his forehead, which meant he had found something, and the look of a joke deciding whether it is in good taste.

“Baldwin. I’ve brought you a present.”

“Is it gasoline?”

“It’s better.” Halloran laid the folder on the G-4 table between a coffee cup and Pike’s elbow and opened it. “It’s a timetable.”

It was. Baldwin knew it before the cover was folded back: the long sheets ruled in blue, the stations down the left, the trains across the top by number, the times in the boxes in the small upright figures of a German railway clerk. Fahrplan. Somebody had spilled something brown on the corner, and somebody else, presumably the same German, had blotted it with great care.

“Off a Reichsbahn liaison office at Alençon,” said Halloran. “The 5th Armored’s G-2 sent it down. They thought it was a code.” He sat on the end of the bench. “I’ve read it all day. It is a plan for running trains over railroads that no longer exist to stations that have been bombed flat at times that have been impossible for a month. And every entry has been revised. In pencil. Some of them four and five times.” He turned a page. “Here. Train 7714, Paris to Alençon via Dreux, fuel and ammunition. Struck out. Rerouted via Chartres. Struck out. Rerouted via Chartres and Nogent. Struck out. And at the bottom, in a different pencil—”

Baldwin read it. “Entfällt.”

“Cancelled. More precisely, ‘falls away.’ Which I think is very fine. I’d like it on my tombstone.”

Pike leaned over and looked at the timetable, and at Halloran, with the face he kept for officers of other services. “This is your present? A German timetable for trains that aren’t running?”

“Yes,” said Halloran. He looked at Baldwin, and something passed between them that had been passing since the orchard at Néhou, since the afternoon Halloran carried a German fuel allocation across the grass as if it were a first edition. “Do you see it?”

“I see it.”

“See what?” said Pike.

“Where they send them.” Baldwin put his finger on the sheet and ran it down the station column, not reading the German but the shape: the places where the pencil gathered, where it struck out and came back, the stations that showed up in the revisions over and over, like a word a man says too often when he is frightened. “Every time they lose a line, they reroute. And every reroute goes back through the same three or four places. Dreux. Chartres. Nogent. Orléans, at the end. They had dumps behind those stations, or they did. They’re falling back on them.” He looked up. “That’s where their gas will be. Whatever they haven’t had time to burn.”

“Huh,” said Pike.

Halloran rose. At the flap he turned. “Oh. You’ve heard? We’ve landed in the south of France. On Tuesday. Saint-Tropez, Saint-Raphaël, those places.” He pushed his spectacles back down. “I find it hard to imagine. I keep picturing them coming ashore in deck chairs.”

Pike watched him go.

“That man is going to get himself shot one day by somebody who’s had no sleep.”

“Not by me.”

“No, Texas,” said Pike, and looked at him, and at the timetable, and at the G-4 board with its single red word. “Not by you. You love him. He brought you a train.”

❦

Lusk had come back that morning.

He came in the back of a ration truck, sitting on a crate of hash with his left arm in a sling and his right arm round a sack of mail, in a hospital pajama top under an OD shirt with a sergeant’s stripes that were not his, and trousers that belonged to a man eight inches shorter, wearing a look of tremendous Oklahoma serenity, like a man arriving early at church.

Baldwin was at the G-4 table with a list of trucks. He watched Lusk climb down one-handed and come across the grass under the apple trees, and for a moment he could not say anything at all.

“Morning, sir.” Lusk saluted with his good right hand, which was the one thing about him that was regulation. “Reporting back.”

“Lusk. What in the name of God.”

“They was going to send me to England, sir.”

“You were shot four days ago.”

“Yes, sir. Through the meat, the doc said. Didn’t touch nothing.” Lusk lowered the salute. “A fella said they were putting the whole ward on a plane today. I said, Lieutenant, ma’am, I can’t go to England, I have to drive the captain. She said I was in no condition. I said I’d been in worse in Okmulgee on a Saturday. She said that wasn’t a medical opinion.” He paused. “She was real pretty. She had freckles all the way up. I don’t believe she’ll speak to me again.”

“Whose shirt is that?”

“I couldn’t say, sir. It was on a chair.”

“Whose trousers?”

“Same chair.”

Pike had come out of the tent. He stood behind Baldwin with his cigar and looked at Lusk’s trousers, which stopped four inches above the ankle and showed a length of pale Oklahoma shin like the leg of a heron, and his face went through a number of expressions and settled on reverence.

“Corporal. How did you get here?”

“Hitched. Le Mans to a ration dump to here. The ration fella didn’t ask.” Lusk considered. “I told him I was mail.”

“You told him you were mail.”

“I had the sack, sir. I figured that made me mail.”

That was when Baldwin laughed. It started somewhere very deep, under the four days and the siding and the fire and Dombrowski, whom he had knelt beside in a burning orchard ten nights ago, under the whole weight of the war since the beaches, and it came up and out, and he sat at the G-4 table with his face in his hands and laughed until the tears ran between his fingers. Pike laughed behind him, the loud Mississippi bray. Szabo came running from the motor pool to see what was wrong and stood with his mouth open. And Lusk, in somebody else’s stripes, stood on the grass with his good hand at his side and waited for them to finish, perfectly patient, his work done and the whole day in front of him.

“Lusk.” Baldwin wiped his eyes. “You are going back. Today. You are going to apologize to the lieutenant with the freckles, and to the man whose trousers those are if he can be found, and you are going to stay there until a doctor says in writing that you can drive. In writing. Then you’ll come back and drive me, and nobody else will, because I won’t let them.”

Something moved in the long face behind the serenity that was not serene at all.

“In writing, sir?”

“In writing.”

“Yes, sir.” Lusk thought about it. “Can Szabo drive you till then?”

“Szabo is driving me now.”

“Sir,” said Lusk, with deep feeling, “Szabo drives like a man trying to get off a horse.”

Szabo spent three words. “I heard that.”

❦

The MPs took Lusk back at two, kindly, in a jeep. He rode in the back with the mail sack on his knees because he would not give it up, and as they drove off he turned in the seat and raised his good hand, and Baldwin raised his. It was not a salute. It was the other thing, the thing a man does at the far end of a clearing.

At a quarter to five, at the railroad yard, where he had gone with Szabo at the wheel and his heart in his mouth at every corner, there came up out of the north on the Mamers line a sound he had been waiting for since Saturday without quite admitting it: a French whistle, two notes, long and short, the sound an engine makes when it wants the signalman to know that it is coming and does not intend to stop.

It came round the curve below the yard at twelve miles an hour, a small black tank engine of a kind Baldwin did not know, old, filthy, with a dent in her side tank and one buffer missing and a tricolor tied to the handrail with a bootlace. Behind her came the tank wagons. He counted them without meaning to, the way he had counted them at four in the morning in the forest in the first gray light: four, eight, twelve, twenty. Twenty-two. And the flats with their drums. Black and wet and dented and unmistakably the train from Les Aulnaies.

A man stood in the cab doorway with one hand on the grab rail and a cold pipe in his teeth, stooped, in a blue jacket gone gray at the elbows, looking down the yard at nothing in particular, the way railwaymen look down yards.

“Monsieur Marchais,” said Baldwin, when the engine had stopped and the steam cleared and he had walked down the platform with his heart doing something he had not authorized. “You said Thursday.”

Marchais took the pipe out and looked at it. “It is Thursday.”

“Where on earth did you find an engine?”

“In a shed at Mamers. Under a great deal of straw.” He climbed down one rung at a time, an old man getting off a train. “The Germans put her there in June to hide her from your airplanes, and then they forgot her. They are very thorough, monsieur. When they forget a thing, they forget it thoroughly.” He knocked the pipe against his heel. “She was built in 1891. She has no business pulling twenty-eight wagons. I told her so. She did not listen. Like my wife.”

Baldwin looked at the little engine, and the long train behind her, and the old man, and found he had nothing to say that would not come out wrong. So he took out his Luckies and offered the pack, and Marchais took one, regarded it with deep suspicion, as he always did, and put it behind his ear, as he always did, for later.

“Will you stay? The Army needs railwaymen. There’ll be a job. There’ll be pay.”

Marchais looked down the yard: at the bombed shed with its iron ribs against the sky, at the platforms swept, at the American railway troops coming down the line with lanterns and clipboards and the cheminots in blue behind them.

“Not now. I have a thing to do at home. At the water tower.” He did not say what thing. Baldwin did not ask. He knew where the water tower stood, in the little station in the Sarthe, and what had happened against it in the spring, and to whom. “Afterward, perhaps. You will be far away. At the Seine. At the Marne. Who knows?” He put the pipe back. “The trains will follow you. They always follow. It is only a matter of who drives them.”

He shook Baldwin’s hand once, hard, a railwayman’s grip, and walked off up the platform and did not look back.

❦

The newspapers came the same afternoon, in a bundle on the mail jeep: a day-old Stars and Stripes, a London Daily Mail, and somebody’s mother’s Cleveland Plain Dealer from three weeks before, which had been circulating in the motor pool and fell open, when Szabo dropped it on the G-4 table, at the sports page.

“Not that one, sir. The Stars and Stripes. Front page.”

Baldwin looked. It said it in letters an inch high, across the front page: PATTON. THIRD ARMY. Supreme Headquarters had announced at last that the Third Army existed, that it was in France, that the general who had spent the spring parked in England in front of a cardboard army was loose on the Continent with a real one, and had been driving for two weeks through Brittany and Maine and the Sarthe while the papers called it “American forces” and “armored columns” and “units of the First Army,” which had driven the general, Pike said, very nearly to drink.

“It says we’re the fastest army in the history of the world,” said Szabo, with deep satisfaction.

“I see that.”

Szabo looked at the paper as if it were a photograph of himself. “Mrs. Dombrowski’s going to get one of these. Somebody’ll send it. She’ll put it up over the bar.” He stopped, which was how he generally ended a sentence in which Dombrowski appeared, and did not say the rest.

Baldwin let him not say it. After a moment he asked, “Is there anything about Falaise?”

Szabo turned the page. There was, on page two, and it was careful, the way papers were careful about things that were not finished: the Germans in Normandy caught in a pocket, the Canadians coming down from the north and the Americans up from the south, the jaws closing, the enemy streaming east through a gap between them under the guns and the airplanes. The gap was still open. Baldwin knew it was open, and where, and that XV Corps had stopped at Argentan on the thirteenth on General Bradley’s order, and that the general had argued with the order and lost, and that men in the G-3 tent had said things about it in the last two days that would have ended careers had anyone written them down. None of that was in the paper. The paper said pocket and jaws and trap. It said the Germans were losing more men and machines than at any time since Stalingrad, which might well have been true.

He folded it and gave it back.

“Is that good, sir? The pocket?”

“It’s good for the ones inside it,” said Baldwin. “I don’t know about the ones who got out. Ask me in a month. They’ll be somewhere. They’ll need gas too.”

Szabo thought about this. He was capable, at intervals, of a silence that changed the air around him, and he was in one now.

“Like the German with the train.”

“Like him.”

“You think he got out?”

“Yes,” said Baldwin. “I think he’s the kind who gets out.”

❦

He wrote to Anne that night in the G-4 tent by the light of a bulb on a cord, while Pike snored on a cot behind a blanket and the typewriters sat under their covers like birds in cages.

He finished the V-Mail he had begun on the platform. He had carried it through the day in his breast pocket with the last line unwritten, and he smoothed it on the table now and read what he had put down, which was nothing, which was the weather and the food and that he was well; and under it, unfinished, Tell Father I am setting it by the—

He wrote dispatcher. He looked at the word because it was the first job in the Army he had ever been proud of.

Then he added a line the censor would leave, because it said nothing at all.

The trains came in. All of them. I watched from a tower with a sergeant from Scranton who had never seen a gold watch and now has, and an old Frenchman brought me twenty-two more this afternoon behind an engine he found under some straw. I don’t think I have ever been so happy in my life, and I can’t tell you why, and you will have to take my word. Kiss Nan. Tell her the answer is still no. I love you. W.

He folded it and put it in the box for the censor, and sat back and lit a cigarette and looked across the dark tent at the G-4 board, the columns, the single red word, and did the sum again in his head.

Ninety miles to Dreux. Ninety to Chartres. More to Orléans. Beyond them the Seine, and beyond the Seine everything else.

Behind the blanket Pike stopped snoring.

“Texas. Go to sleep.”

“I’m thinking.”

“I can hear you. It sounds like a Presbyterian counting the collection.” The cot creaked. “Tomorrow the general’s going to want to know how the gas gets to the Seine. And you’re going to tell him. And it won’t be the answer he wants. So go to sleep, so you can tell him with your eyes open.”

Baldwin put out the cigarette. He did not go to sleep. He sat in the dark with his father’s watch open in his hand, listening to it and to the trucks on the road beyond the orchard going east, all night, one behind another, lights out, and he thought: every one of them has to come back.

Somewhere ahead of them, at the end of all that road, the general’s arrows were still going.
