Chapter 1: Treadway

By John W. Davis. About 3,300 words, roughly 13 minutes.

The Rhine at Oppenheim, March 28, 1945

The river was brown.

Deacon Hollis had been told since the seventh grade that it was blue. A teacher in Toomsuba, a Miss Ransome, who had a map of Europe pinned to the wall of the colored school and a pointer made from a curtain rod, had shown the class where it ran, and had said that the Germans sang about it, and the song made it blue. Deacon had believed her. For twelve years he had carried a blue river in his head, narrow and clean, with castles. Now it lay in front of the truck from one side of the windshield to the other, a reach of slow brown water, with a skin of oil on it and a drowned door turning in the current, and he laughed out loud.

“What?” said Dub. He was in the right seat, where he had ridden all week while Ike’s truck waited on Peabody and a spring, with a cigarette cupped in his hand, the way he smoked when the road was in the open.

“It’s the wrong color.”

“The river?”

“I was told blue.”

“Who told you?”

“My teacher.”

“Then your teacher never been here.” Dub leaned to look. “That’s a good river, though. I’ll say that. That’s a river a man could be proud of, if he was a river. Mama’s going to want to know what color it is. I’ll write her brown. She’ll think I made a mistake.”

The column had stopped a quarter mile short of the water, in a long gray line on the approach road, with the engines idling and the exhaust hanging in the cold. It was the morning of the twenty-eighth of March, the fourth day of XYZ, the new through-haul service, and the company had been on the road since a little past three. They had loaded at Luxembourg in the dark. They had come up through the Palatinate behind a tank destroyer outfit that went at twelve miles an hour and would not pull over, and in the first light they had seen what the winter had left of the country west of the river: a village with its steeple down, a bridge in the creek bed, a field with six burned trucks in a row, painted with a white star, and the farmers already out in the furrows behind the burned trucks, plowing.

By the evening of the twenty-third the engineers had a treadway bridge across. Now Deacon could see the trucks moving on it.

A strange thing to look at. He had expected a bridge in the way that he knew bridges, a span of stone or iron lifted above the water on piers. This one lay on the river like a belt. It was a roadway of steel treads, two long tracks wide enough for the wheels of a truck and no wider, laid on rubber pontoons that rose and fell with the current, so that the whole length of it moved a little, as the back of a sleeping animal moves. He could see the pontoons under it. They were dark, and low in the water, and they bobbed. At the near end a sign had been painted on a board and nailed to a post, in white letters a foot high on a field of black, and he read it over twice.

10 MPH. 100 YDS. NO PASSING. NO HALTING. IF YOU STOP, YOU WILL BE MOVED.

“They mean it,” said Dub.

“I can see that.”

“What’s that last part?”

“It means if you stall they push you in.”

“That’s an Engineer sign.” Dub put out the cigarette on the sole of his boot. “That’s a sign written by a man who has had a bad week.”

They went across at nine.

An engineer sergeant in a yellow slicker stood at the bank with a flag, a short wide man with a red face who had been standing there so long that the mud was over his ankles. He took the trucks one at a time. He counted them with the flag. He raised it, and the first truck of the first serial rolled onto the steel, and the whole bridge settled under it a hand’s breadth into the river and sighed; and he lowered the flag, and counted a hundred yards of silence with his lips; and raised it again.

It took the company four hours.

Deacon’s turn came at ten minutes past noon. He sat with the engine in low and his foot light on the clutch and watched the truck ahead of him, which was Greer’s, go down the bank onto the first of the treads. He had thought about it for a week, and now that it was in front of him it was quite small. He saw the tailgate go away from him across the water, steady, with the number stenciled on it, and a jerrican on the bumper, and Greer’s arm out of the cab window, hanging. The sergeant brought the flag down. He counted. He raised it.

“Go on,” said Dub quietly.

He let the clutch out.

The treads took the weight. He felt it come up through the wheel into his wrists, a long slow tremble, as though the truck had been set down on the skin of a drum, and then the bank was behind him and he was moving over the water at ten miles an hour with the river on both sides. He avoided looking at it. He looked at the tailgate of Greer’s truck, a hundred yards ahead, and he held his speed at ten and his hands at the places on the wheel where Peabody had told him to hold them, and he did not brake. There was a place in the middle where the roadway dipped. He felt it go down under the rear axle, and come up again, and he thought of a bridge in Belgium on a night in January, and of a stone, and of a boy named Cleophus Rowe, who used to slap the dash twice before a hard pull. Rowe had signed his rule on the back of an order sheet, and had put his foot to the floor to make it.

He took his hand off the wheel and put it flat on the dash, a moment, and put it back.

“You all right?” said Dub.

“Yes.”

“You want me to drive?”

“No.”

“I’d let you sing.”

“I know,” said Deacon. “I’m not going to.”

“Pruitt said—”

“I know what he said.”

It had been said on a cold night on a hill, to a boy at a fire, and it had been said with some care. Deacon had thought about it for six weeks. He had come to see that it was not an order. It was a request, from a man who did not often make them, that he not put a song between himself and the road when the road was dangerous; and he had decided, privately, that he would keep it for as long as it was asked, and no longer. That left many places in the day for a song. There was the waiting at the dump. There was the evening. There was the hour before the rest, when the light came down yellow on the trucks and the men sat on the running boards and nobody wanted to talk. But the bridge was not one of them. He kept his mouth shut and the river went by under him, brown and slow, and for the length of a thousand feet of water he heard nothing but the treads.

Then there was a bump, and the bank, and the engineer on the far side with a different flag. The road went up from the water into a street of broken houses. Deacon found that he had been holding the wheel as if it might get away from him, and let go a little.

“Well,” said Dub. “That’s the Rhine.”

“I thought there would be more to it.”

“I thought a bridge.” Dub took out another cigarette, and looked at it, and put it back in the pack. “I’m going to tell you something. I was scared the whole way.”

“So was I.”

“Were you?”

“I thought it’d go down.”

“I know you did. I could tell by your ears.” He grinned. “They go back.”

In the afternoon, in the village by the dump, Greer found the cellar.

It lay under the inn, behind a door the size of a coffin lid, and what it held was a row of oak kegs on their sides in the cool, each as fat as a hog, with chalk marks on the heads. Greer found it by following the smell. He came up the steps again, slowly, with his hat in his hand and a look on his face that Deacon had seen on him only once before, in a church.

“Gentlemen,” he said. “I want it understood that I did not touch anything.”

“What is it?” said Dub.

“I am not at liberty to say. It is under the inn. It is round, and it is wooden, and it is not going anywhere without a truck.”

Dub went down to see. He was gone four minutes. When he came back he was walking carefully, with an egg in each pocket, and he said in a low voice that the matter was a question of salvage, that the Army had abandoned any claim to the contents by driving past them, and that a keg was a very small thing to leave to a country that had started all this.

Banks was consulted. He stood at the top of the cellar steps with his black ledger and said that to his knowledge there was no regulation covering a keg, there being no previous instance, and that in the absence of a regulation the only honest course was to enter it. He wrote: One (1) keg, beer, German, contents unknown. Then, after thought: contents suspected.

They had it up the steps by three o’clock, four men on a plank, and into the back of Dub’s truck under a tarpaulin, where it sat among the crates with the dignity of a visiting bishop. It was at that moment that Pruitt came round the corner of the inn with his hands behind him.

He avoided looking at the tarpaulin. He looked at the sky, at the pines, at the steeple of the village church, and finally, for a good while, at Dub, who had arranged himself against the tailgate with the expression of a man who has never in his life been near a barrel.

“Staff Sergeant Cole.”

“Top.”

“I am told there is a cellar in this village that has been condemned.”

“Condemned, Top?”

“As unsafe for troops.” Pruitt’s voice did not alter. “The floor is damp, and the contents are of enemy origin. I have posted a guard.” He turned his head. “Private Banks.”

“First Sergeant.”

“You will take an inventory of the cellar and report to me at supper. Take as long as you find necessary.”

“Of the contents, First Sergeant?”

“Of the contents that are there,” said Pruitt, “when you arrive.”

He went away round the inn. They stood in a row and watched him go, five men in the thin March sun with their mouths slightly open. Greer said it. He said it very softly, to nobody. “Lord, he’s got a mind.”

At supper Pruitt asked whether his guard had been relieved.

“Not yet,” said Greer.

“See to it. Staff Sergeant Cole has a run at three.”

Dub, who had eaten standing beside the tarpaulin, brought his plate back to the cookhouse and demanded to know whose beer it was now.

The dump was at a place called Groß-Gerau, which Deacon wrote in the back of his Bible on the flyleaf because he thought he would want it, and spelled twice before he got it right. It lay in a pine wood short of Groß-Gerau, behind a screen of camouflage netting that had been strung there by somebody else, and the Germans had left a good part of it. The men unloading found a staff car, upside down, with a dog sleeping in it, and a wall of freshly sawn planks, stacked and numbered, that had been meant for a bridge and was now going to be used as one.

The trucks stood in the pines and cooled. The drivers sat on the tailgates and ate out of cans.

It was a still, bright, cold afternoon, the first of the spring, with a haze on the trees, and from where Deacon sat on the running board of his truck with a can of hash on his knee he could hear, a long way off to the east, the guns, which had been going for a week and had become part of the quiet. They were farther away now than they had been at Metz. They were going away at a steady pace, as the sound of a train goes, and he found himself listening to them with a faint private regret, like a man hearing the last of a party from the next street.

Banks came across the pine needles with his black book under his arm. He sat on the ground. He was a thin man and he sat as thin men do, with his knees up and his spectacles pushed back on his forehead, and he did not say anything for a minute.

“I counted the pontoons,” he said.

“How many?”

“Ninety-one. It’s an approximate figure. Several were under the treads and I took the spacing.” He pulled the spectacles down. “It is a Treadway bridge. I looked it up. The engineers have a manual. It will take a loaded truck, if the speed is kept, and it is wide enough for a truck of this company and not a hair wider. I thought you might like to know.”

“Why?”

“Because you were holding the wheel as if it were hot.”

Deacon was silent.

“It’s all right,” said Banks. “I did too. I have never been so conscious of my own weight.” He considered. “It is a curious thing, the Rhine. The Romans stopped at it. Caesar built a bridge across it in ten days. We had that at school. I was thinking of that when we were on the middle span. I was thinking that nobody has ever driven a truck across it without having been told it could not be done.”

“Who told you that?”

“Nobody. I deduced it.” Banks took off the spectacles and rubbed them on his coat. “The Army told us that the Rhine was the end. At Thionville they said it would be the end of the war. Then they said there would be a river at the Elbe. There is always another one. It rests me.”

“Restful.”

“A man who has a river to cross does not have to ask what comes after it.”

Deacon opened the Bible. It was a small black book with a cracked back and the corners worn to the cloth, and he opened it, as he did most evenings, by letting it fall where it wished. It fell at the Book of Joshua. He read, in the haze, in the mild afternoon, the third chapter, where the people of Israel came to the Jordan at the time of harvest, when it overflowed its banks, and the priests who bore the ark put their feet in the brim of the water, and the water stood in a heap.

He read to the end of the chapter. Then he closed the book. He said nothing about it to Banks, who was not a man who would have wanted him to.

“Which was it?” said Banks.

“Joshua.”

“Do they cross?”

“On dry ground,” said Deacon. “All of them.”

“That’s a good ending.”

“It’s a bad bridge, though,” said Deacon. “It doesn’t say anything about the weight.”

Banks laughed, and a lieutenant who was passing with a clipboard turned his head and looked at the two of them in some alarm, as if they had said something against the Corps of Engineers.

❦

They loaded again at four, from a pile of crates beside a railway spur that had been repaired with the help of the Germans in the village. It was pleasant work; the weather was good and there was no hurry. The prisoners who did the loading, a squad of gray men with their caps in their hands, moved the boxes onto the tailgates in silence, with the care of men who have been told that they will be fed if they do. Deacon watched them. They were not as he had imagined. The war had given him an enemy with a face like a cut stone, and the men at the spur were old and young and ordinary, in ill-fitting coats, and one of them, a big man with a bald head and a missing finger, picked up a dropped crate at the tailgate and set it square, and touched his forehead as he would have to a foreman.

Deacon touched his own. The man looked up, startled. A smile started, and went out, and he lowered his eyes and went on with the work.

He put it away, and drove.

At the river there was a different sergeant at the bridge. He was tall and thin, and he held up the flag, and counted. The sun was going down. It lay along the water in a long flat sheet of copper, and every pontoon had its own small fire on the side that faced it, and the bridge, which had been a dull gray belt at noon, ran across the river now like a road of lit coals, to the western bank and the dark.

“Pretty,” said Dub, from the right seat.

“It is.”

“I’d like to remember it.”

“You will.”

“No, I mean I’d like to write it down. Ike does that. I never knew why. I’d always thought it was a way of not looking.” He leaned his head against the glass. “But you can look, and then you can write it. The two don’t fight.”

“What would you put?”

Dub thought. The truck climbed the first of the treads and the bridge sank under it, with a sigh, into the river.

“Brown,” he said. “Gold at night. Crossed twice. Nobody went in.”

❦

Deacon’s entry, March 28, 1945.

He kept a diary of his own, in the Bible, in the margins and the blank leaves at the back, in a small upright hand that his father had taught him with a ruler. The book was not a record of loads. It was a record of what he had read and where. He wrote:

Joshua 3. Rhine, Oppenheim, noon. Brown. Eleven hundred feet, about. Did not sing. Heard the treads. Thought of R. Everyone across.

Under it, after a while, he added a line, because the day was not finished and the lamp was lit:

The man at the spur touched his cap.

Then he put out the lamp, and lay on the cot in the cold tent with the book on his chest, and listened to the guns going away to the east, and slept.
