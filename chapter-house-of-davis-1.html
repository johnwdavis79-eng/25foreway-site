Chapter 1: The Counter on Market Street

By John W. Davis. About 1,700 words, roughly 7 minutes.

Before there was an international trophy, there was a counter.

It stood on Market Street in St. Louis, in a small store near the old Merchants’ Exchange. A young man from Brookline, Massachusetts, had gone into the dry-goods trade there with a partner named Stanford. Their trade was “barter as well as sale.”

Goods were measured. Credit was extended or refused. Debts were recorded and, when they went unpaid, pursued, in one surviving instance by a pair of Springfield lawyers named Lincoln and Herndon. The house would grow large enough to occupy what its admirers called “the best dry goods building in the United States.”

Samuel Craft Davis was born on January 18, 1809, a son of Robert Sharp Davis and Lucy Stearns, and baptized in Brookline on May 7. His father died in August 1821, when the boy was twelve. He survives the way a customer survives in a merchant’s books, as a column of entries.

The St. Louis Globe-Democrat, writing sixty years later, put the family’s situation in the polite language of the Gilded Age: “his parents being in moderate circumstances, he began life without any other advantages than a good education, self reliance, excellent business qualities and a rare energy and perfect integrity.”

“About 1832,” the obituary continues, “Mr. Davis came West. For a short time he was located at Quincy, Ill., but his keen foresight led him to come here and engage in business.” Quincy was then a raw settlement on the Illinois bank of the Mississippi, 160 miles by water above St. Louis.

St. Louis in the early 1830s held perhaps six or seven thousand people, packed along a narrow strip of high ground above the levee. It was still a French town in its street plan and its oldest families, and a frontier depot in its business; the fur trade of the Chouteaus and their partners remained its signature enterprise. Steamboats had been arriving since the Zebulon M. Pike docked in August 1817, and by the 1830s they crowded the landing, because the shallower channel above the city made it the place where cargoes passed between the big lower-river boats and the lighter craft of the upper Mississippi and the Missouri. By 1840 the population stood at 16,469.

J. C. Wild’s lithographs of 1840 and 1841 give the look of the landing in those years: the levee sloping in stone and mud to a wall of steamboat hulls, boats moored bow-in, stacks above hogsheads, barrels, bales and crates. Goods came up from New Orleans and over the mountains from Philadelphia, Baltimore and New York by way of Pittsburgh and the Ohio; furs, lead, hides and produce went down. A merchant’s place in that traffic was set by the distance between his door and the water. The first Davis store stood close to it.

Beside the obituary’s “Market street, near the old Merchants’ Exchange,” the guidebook of 1878, A Tour of St. Louis, puts it “in a little store at Market and Commercial streets, then the business center,” and names his partner as J. R. Stanford. Commercial Street, in the old grid, ran just above the river; Market Street climbed westward from it. The barter trade, the guidebook adds, “was profitable nevertheless, and, what is of more consequence, it grew steadily with the city.”

❦

The firm’s later history dated its founding to January 19, 1835, and named three founders: David R. Griggs, J. R. Stanford and Samuel C. Davis.

By the end of the decade Davis was a partner in two firms. The St. Louis directory compiled for 1840–41 lists “Stanford & Davis, grocers and drygoods merchants, 7 Market,” and, separately, “Davis, Tilden & Richards, dealers in drygoods and groceries, 49 Market.” The first firm was the original partnership; the second joined Davis with John Tilden and Eben Richards, the men the Tour of St. Louis names as his partners after Stanford “retired after a short time.” Both firms dealt in groceries as well as cloth.

On August 13, 1840, at Brookline, Samuel Craft Davis married Caroline Tilden of that town. The couple had two sons: Samuel Jr., the elder, and John Tilden Davis, born in St. Louis on September 13, 1844.

❦

In May 1837 the banks stopped paying. On May 10 the banks of New York suspended payment in specie; Boston and New Orleans followed on May 12 and 13. One institution conspicuously refused to suspend: the Bank of the State of Missouri at St. Louis, which kept paying hard money while others did not. The depression that followed lasted, with interruptions, into the early 1840s, and it ended many mercantile careers.

The same streets held another kind of commerce. Missouri had entered the Union as a slave state in 1821. Enslaved people lived and worked throughout St. Louis as domestic servants, artisans, deckhands and wharf laborers, and they were bought and sold in the city’s commercial district. Bernard M. Lynch ran a slave market on Locust Street between Fourth and Fifth; his “pens” were former houses with bars across the windows, to which people were brought from the steamboats and from which they were sold. Court-ordered sales of enslaved people, as part of the property of estates and bankrupts, were held on the steps of the courthouse. A National Park Service study of the St. Louis Probate Court records counted 533 individuals who appear in the court documents as offered for sale on the courthouse steps, and 724 named in connection with court-ordered sales; the count excludes every private auction and private sale in the city.

The river trade and Missouri country stores in which the firm prospered depended in part on enslaved labor. By 1878 the house’s customers extended through Kentucky, Tennessee, Arkansas, Louisiana and Texas, thirteen years after Missouri abolished slavery.

On April 28, 1836, Francis L. McIntosh, a free Black man of about twenty-six from Pittsburgh, a porter and cook on the steamboat Flora, was arrested on the levee after refusing to help two officers seize some sailors. Told he faced years in jail, he stabbed both officers, killing one. A white mob broke into the jail, dragged him to a tree, chained him there and burned him alive. One account puts the tree near what is now Seventh and Chestnut; another, at Tenth and Market. No one was indicted.

Elijah P. Lovejoy, who had condemned the lynching in his St. Louis Observer, was driven across the river to Alton, Illinois, where a crowd shot him dead on November 7, 1837. Nearly three months later a young Springfield lawyer named Abraham Lincoln told the Young Men’s Lyceum that the “horror-striking scene at St. Louis” showed how mob law could destroy a republic: McIntosh, Lincoln said, “was seized in the street, dragged to the suburbs of the city, chained to a tree, and actually burned to death; and all within a single hour from the time he had been a freeman, attending to his own business, and at peace with the world.”

In 1882 the Globe-Democrat described Davis, after his death, as “a member of the Second Baptist Church and a strict follower of that sect.” It credits him with “perfect integrity” and adds that Davis “gave often, but unostentatiously, shunning all self-glorifying display.”

The obituary gives him a character; Eliot’s notebook records a refusal. When the Unitarian minister William Greenleaf Eliot, in the 1850s, sought to make him a director of the new Washington University, he declined. Eliot’s notebook preserves the appeal he sent: the founders hoped to make the school “the leading Univ[ersit]y of the West,” Eliot wrote, and “your position in this community and your … energy of character will greatly aid in its accomplishment.” Eliot added a second reason, more personal — he was “particularly desirous” that Davis’s sons should remain at the university rather than go east, “because it will establish a precedent that others will follow.” The sons did remain; John Tilden Davis graduated from Washington University in 1863. The father did not join the board. The gift the obituary does name went to his own congregation: $10,000 to the building fund of its new church, which, the paper noted, he “never saw.”

❦

By the late 1840s the house of Davis, Tilden & Richards had outlived the Stanford partnership and grown with the city. The federal census of 1850 counted 77,860 people. The year before, the city had suffered two catastrophes in quick succession.

The first was cholera. Boats from New Orleans brought sick passengers and dead ones in January; by spring the disease was established, and by the end of the year it had killed at least 4,317 people by one modern count, and probably more. A contemporary journal counted 4,285 cholera deaths among 8,445 from all causes. The city’s death register did not begin until 1850, and many victims were buried beyond the city limits.

The second came on the night of May 17. The steamboat White Cloud, moored at the foot of Cherry Street, was burning. Crewmen had put out a mattress fire, aired the bedding on deck and dragged it back into a cabin, missing an ember. The alarm sounded that evening. The volunteer firemen came with nine hand engines and hose reels, but the flames spread to a neighboring boat, the Bates, which burned free of its mooring and drifted down the crowded landing. Twenty-three boats burned, and some fifteen blocks of the business district east of Third Street.

The Tour of St. Louis gives the outcome for the house in one sentence: “The great fire of 1849, the most sweeping conflagration that has ever visited St. Louis, just stopped short of their house in its destructive course.”

The burned district was rebuilt in brick and iron, four and five stories high. The Tour goes on to note that “changes in the firm from time to time occasionally occurred, but the controlling interest always remained with Mr. Davis, and his active and sagacious mind directed the operations of the business.” Within a few years the house would carry his name alone.

The 1878 guidebook also described the firm’s later building on Washington Avenue. The basement of the new building, the Tour reports, was “provided with fire-proof vaults, in which the old books and accounts of the firm are preserved.”

Their present whereabouts are unknown.
