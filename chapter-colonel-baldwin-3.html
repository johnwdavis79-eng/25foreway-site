Chapter 1: General Muller

By John W. Davis. About 3,800 words, roughly 15 minutes.

Nancy — December 4, 1944

It had rained on Nancy for nineteen days. It rained on the gilt gates of the Place Stanislas and on the statue of the old duke in the middle of it, and on the trucks parked nose to tail under the arcades, and on the sentries, who stood in it with their collars up and water running off their helmets into their necks. The gutters of the Rue Saint-Dizier ran brown. In the offices of Third Army’s G-4 section, on the third floor of a requisitioned house off the Place de la Carrière, several streets from Army headquarters, the windows sweated and the radiators knocked and did very little, and the clerks typed in gloves with the fingers cut off, and every map on every wall had curled at the corners with the damp.

The star arrived before the general did.

It came in a small cardboard box from a jeweler on the rue Saint-Dizier, wrapped in tissue, and it was carried up the stairs of the G-4 section by Corporal Floyd Lusk of Okmulgee, Oklahoma, with his left arm in a sling and the box held out before him in his right hand, like an usher bringing the collection plate to a congregation that had not given before. He set it on Captain Baldwin’s desk. He stepped back, leaving his good hand extended toward the box.

“What is that,” said Baldwin.

“A star, sir.”

“I can see it is a star. One star. What is it doing on my desk?”

“General Muller as of today, sir. It’s in the order. Third Army has got him a star, and the General hasn’t got one, on account of the Army not issuing them to a man till the paper comes through, and the paper’s coming through on the train from Paris with a major who’s stopped in Reims to see a woman. So I went to the jeweler’s.”

Baldwin looked at the box. He opened it. There lay a five-pointed silver star the size of a half-dollar, of excellent workmanship, on a pin. It had been made, he saw, for the collar of a Frenchman’s idea of an American general, and it was a little too grand, with a fine edge to every point, like a sheriff’s badge in a picture-book.

“How much?”

“The jeweler says it’s a gift, sir. To the liberators.”

“Nobody in Nancy gives a general anything without sending the bill to the captain.” Baldwin shut the lid. “How much, Lusk?”

Lusk studied the ceiling. It was a good ceiling, a high plaster ceiling in the old Lorraine fashion, with a plaster nymph who had lost her nose in some earlier war and now regarded the room with the contemptuous calm of a woman who has seen it before.

“Eleven cartons, sir,” he said at length. “And a chaplain’s blanket.”

“Whose chaplain?”

“A Lutheran, sir. He didn’t need it. He’s from Minnesota.”

It was a quarter to nine on a gray Monday morning, and the section had been up since five. On the floor below, the typewriters went like hail on a tin roof. Through the tall windows the rain crossed the Place de la Carrière in slanting sheets. The plaster wreaths above the windows had darkened with damp. The Third Army had taken over the town in September and had not yet got used to it. It was a city of balconies and cream-colored stone. Every other building had been built to hold a king, and was now holding a quartermaster.

Baldwin put the box in his pocket.

“Where is Major Pike?”

“At the dentist, sir.”

“On the fourth of December. At nine in the morning.”

“Yes, sir. He says it’s the cigar, sir. He says it’s wearing a groove.”

He had been a captain throughout his time with Third Army, and he had never in that time seen Colonel Muller surprised.

The Colonel could be pleased. He could be disappointed, by a gradation so fine that only the section understood it, as the fishermen of the Chesapeake understand a change in the wind. He could be angry, though the anger in him went in, like water into sand, and showed itself only in a certain stillness of the hands. But surprise happened to other men. A bridge went down, a train was lost, a general changed his mind at four in the afternoon about where eleven thousand tons were to go; Muller listened, looked at the map for a moment as though it had said something rather clever, and said, “How much, where, and by when.”

It was the whole of his method. The section had come to believe that if the Germans had once asked him that question, he would have supplied the answer.

At nine o’clock he came up the stairs.

He took them two at a time, which at forty-nine was an affair of some dignity, and arrived at the landing with a small gray face and a briefcase and the look of a man examining a bill. He was a thin, spare, dry officer, with spectacles, and a mouth that closed like the clasp of a purse. He wore the eagles of a colonel. He looked at them, as he went past the mirror in the hall, with an expression of faint dislike, as one might look at the face of a distant relative who had been a nuisance in the family.

“Good morning, Baldwin.”

“Good morning, sir.”

“You are standing. Why are you standing?”

“The section is told that you’ve been promoted, sir.”

“The section is told a great many things by Corporal Lusk.” The General hung his coat on the nail. There was a brief silence. “The telegram came at six. The paper itself will be here Thursday, or when the major has finished with his woman.” He put the briefcase beside his chair. “I do not intend to discuss it. I am told that a brigadier general is expected to be photographed. I intend to refuse.”

“Yes, sir.”

“I intend also to be on time to the forenoon conference, with the figures for the Saar. How are the figures for the Saar?”

“The figures for the Saar, sir, are done.”

The General nodded.

It was the first of the day. Baldwin noted the time.

❦

It was the cake that undid everybody.

The idea had come, as the good ideas of the section generally did, from the lowest rank. Lusk had spoken of it at the mess in the form of a rumor, which he had then confirmed, in the next sentence, as a plan. A man ought to be marked, he said. He had been to a good many promotions, in the Oklahoma National Guard, and there was always a cake, and a cake was the thing a man remembered when the speeches were done. It need not be large. It need only be, as he put it, “of the occasion.”

“Of the occasion,” said Halloran, looking up from a stack of captured German railway maps. He took his pipe out of his mouth. He was a lanky, stooped lieutenant of the G-2 attachment, with thick glasses and a Philadelphia Quaker’s way of agreeing so politely that one knew he was going to take it apart. “What is the occasion, Corporal? That a man has been promoted for the exercise of arithmetic?”

“That he’s a general, sir.”

“A Friend does not, strictly, hold with the idea of rank.”

“Neither does the Army, sir,” said Lusk, “but it keeps a lot of it.”

So the cake was required, and the question was where to get it. There was no flour in Nancy. There had been flour in Nancy for the third week of September, in the sense that it was lying about in the cellars of the Wehrmacht’s ration depot, but the section had taken the flour for the troops, and the troops had eaten it, and the French had been left with what the French always have been left with, which is ingenuity. One bakery on the rue des Dominicains was said to bake at night. It was owned by a Madame Gaudin, a widow of sixty, with a flour burn along one wrist and a daughter of nineteen who was variously described.

Baldwin went himself, with Lusk, at four in the afternoon, in a jeep.

The shop was dim and cold and smelled of yeast and coal smoke. A bell on a spring announced them. At the back, behind a counter, a woman of sixty looked up from a ledger and said, in a flat and carrying voice, that there was no bread.

“We don’t want bread, madame,” said Baldwin, in the French he had been assembling since August, a word at a time. “We want a cake.”

“There is no cake.”

“It is for a general.”

“There is certainly no cake for a general,” said Madame Gaudin. “There are generals in this town like cockroaches. One wishes them every kind of happiness, but one does not feed them.”

“It is for a particular general. A thin one. He does not eat much.”

“Then he does not need a cake.”

A step on the stair behind her. The daughter appeared, in an apron, with flour to the elbows, and a lovely, much-amused face. She looked at Lusk. Lusk studied the ceiling, his habit in peril, and the sling on his arm gave him, Baldwin thought, the air of a wounded stork.

“Maman,” said the girl, “the little one with the arm. He gave the Saint-Sébastien baby a pair of shoes in October.”

“That was the other one.”

“It was this one. He has the same face.”

“All Americans have the same face. It is a national weakness.”

“Maman—”

It took twenty minutes. It took, at the end of them, three cartons of Lucky Strikes, a tin of coffee, a tin of lard, two bars of soap, and an assurance that the Americans would not at any time in the future take the cellar. It also took three low words from Lusk across the counter that Baldwin did not catch, in a language that was part French and part Oklahoma, and which resulted in the daughter going the color of a ripe tomato and the mother saying, in her flat voice, “You will bring him back for the Fête of the Immaculate Conception, and we will see.”

“Yes, madame.”

“And you will sit on the chair that I give you.”

“Yes, madame.”

They went out into the dusk, with the cake in a hatbox, and Baldwin waited until they had gone ten paces before he asked.

“What did you say to her?”

“Sir?”

“To the daughter.”

“I said,” said Lusk, with great dignity, “that I was a Baptist and a man of honest intention.”

“And what did she say?”

“She said she would like to see the intention, sir.”

Baldwin stopped in the street. He did not mean to. The laugh came out of him all at once and rather loudly, a large sound for the Rue des Dominicains in the dusk, and a man leaning in a doorway turned to look. He leaned on the jeep. Lusk looked at him with a polite and injured expression. “That’s one hundred and thirteen, sir,” he said, “if she remembers me at the Fête.”

“It is a hundred and twelve.”

“Yes, sir. Provisional.”

❦

The hatbox rode back on Baldwin’s knee, and it was the road that nearly ruined it.

The distance from the rue des Dominicains to the Army building was perhaps a mile, and in ordinary times would have been covered in five minutes. But the roads of Nancy in December were a kind of slow-moving parade of the American machine, with every vehicle in the Third Army appearing to wish to go through the same square at the same moment, in contrary directions. There was a military policeman at the Place Carnot. He had a white helmet, white gloves, and a gaze of such profound and ancient suspicion that he might have been a customs officer in a more frightened century.

“Hatbox,” said the MP.

“Yes.”

“Where’s it going?”

“To a general.”

“What’s in it?”

“A cake.”

The MP considered this. He was a tall man from Alabama, as the voice declared, and he looked at the hatbox as a fisherman looks at the water. “Sir,” he said, “is it a cake for a good general?”

“I believe so.”

“Because I got a general last week on the Rue Saint-Jean who wouldn’t give a man a match.” He stepped back. He waved them on. “Carry it level.”

At the last gate the sentry, a boy of nineteen or so, took a long look at the hatbox, a longer one at the captain, and said with enormous feeling, in the tone of a man at the end of a hard winter, “Sir, I’ll give you my watch for a slice.”

“It’s a general’s cake.”

“I don’t want the general’s. I just want to smell it.”

Baldwin held it down to him. The boy leaned over and put his nose to the lid and shut his eyes, and the rain ran off his helmet, and he stood a moment, breathing, a boy from a farm in Iowa or Ohio, three thousand miles from the smell of his mother’s kitchen. Then he straightened, and saluted, and said “Thank you, sir,” and the jeep went in. Neither of them said a word for the length of the courtyard.

❦

They held it in the corner office at six, with the door shut, and the shutters closed, and the blackout curtain pinned, so that the gray old room with its Louis XV desk and its map of the Saar looked like the cabin of a ship.

There were six of them. Pike had returned from the dentist, with a swollen jaw and the Palermo cigar unlit in the corner of his mouth, where it had been for as long as the section had known him. It was a long brown cigar of a good, dark leaf, gone dry and a little frayed at one end, and he carried it, as Baldwin had once been told, in the same pocket as his Mississippi draft card. He had bought it in Palermo in the summer of 1943 from a man selling them off a tray, and had not smoked it. He said he was saving it for a particular day.

“Which day?” Baldwin had asked once.

“Day I’m sitting on my own porch, Texas. In Greenville. In a chair my daddy built. With a drink in the left hand and nothing at all to do in the right.” Pike had looked at the cigar. “Then I’ll light her. Not one hour before.”

He said it to the cigar rather than the man, in a low Delta drawl, with a faint wistfulness that he would have denied under oath. The section had come to treat it as a form of liturgy. Lusk said it was the only promise in the Third Army that was certain to be kept.

Halloran sat on the window sill with the pipe. Lusk stood by the door with the hatbox, and a Technician named Gunderson, from the typing pool, held the cake knife, a bayonet. The General himself stood at the map, with his back to them, apparently engaged in contemplating Metz.

“Sir,” said Baldwin.

Muller turned.

He saw the box. He saw the faces. For a moment, as he stood there with the lamp on his spectacles, it seemed that something might actually take place, and no one moved. Then he took off his glasses, and wiped them, and put them on again, and spoke in the dry, level tone with which he had been known to dismiss an army corps.

“I said I did not intend to be photographed.”

“There’s no camera, sir,” said Lusk. “Just the cake.”

“Corporal, I have been in the Army since the last war. I have seen cakes.”

“Not this one, sir.”

Lusk lifted the lid.

It was a big round sponge, of the sort made in the provinces of France with what was available, and the French had had very little available. It stood on a cardboard circle, golden-brown and a little uneven, with a thick white icing made of heaven knew what, and on the top, in cut-up bits of candied orange peel, was a star. Not an elaborate star. A somewhat lopsided, five-pointed star, the work of a girl of nineteen who had had to cut the points freehand. One of them was shorter than the others. It was a star, however, and no one in the room could have mistaken it for anything else.

There was a silence. It was the stillness of the hands, Baldwin saw; the General’s hands had gone quite still at his sides.

“It is a very fair likeness,” said Halloran, gravely, “of the insignia.”

The General stepped forward. He bent. He looked at the cake closely, and at the star, as if it were a document he proposed to initial, and he put out one finger and touched the icing, and put the finger to his mouth.

The room waited.

“Good,” said Muller.

He said it softly. It was not the Good of the figures and the junctions. It had something odd in it, a quality that Baldwin had not heard before, and he realized after a moment that the General’s voice had cracked on the vowel, very slightly, so that the word had come out smaller than he meant it to. Muller cleared his throat. He took out a handkerchief and blew his nose, with force, as though a man had spoken rudely to him.

“The Army,” he said, “has a regulation against the issue of cake to general officers.”

“Yes, sir.”

“I will not enforce it today.” He took the knife. “Baldwin, where is the star?”

“Here, sir.”

“The proper one. Not the orange peel.”

Baldwin took out the box. The General opened it, and regarded the little silver star, the Frenchman’s star, with its sheriff’s edge and its fine points, for a moment. He held it up to the lamp. “Where did this come from?”

“A jeweler, sir. On the rue Saint-Dizier.”

“What did it cost?”

“Eleven cartons, sir,” said Lusk, “and a chaplain’s blanket.”

“A chaplain’s—”

“A Lutheran, sir. He didn’t need it.”

Pike made a noise in his throat that began as a cough and ended as a wheeze, and turned his face to the wall. Halloran found something to do with his pipe. Gunderson dropped the bayonet. And Brigadier General Walter J. Muller, who had been in the Army since the last war and had never been seen to laugh, stood in the middle of the room with a silver star in one hand and a knife in the other and the corner of his mouth doing something it had no authority to do.

“I will pin it on myself,” he said, “tonight. In private. Before the conference.” He cut the cake. It took some time; he cut it in even slices, as he did everything, using the ruler of his own eye. “Corporal Lusk will have the first piece. Because he is wounded.”

“I’m not wounded, sir. It’s the old shoulder.”

“Then because he is a thief.”

“Yes, sir,” said Lusk, with deep satisfaction. “Thank you, sir.”

❦

They had the last of it at nine. The others had gone. The rain had stopped, and the great window of the corner office, with its blackout curtain half drawn, showed a strip of wet street, a gleam of lamplight on the cobbles, and the dark shape of a sentry walking his beat.

Baldwin was at his desk with the Saar figures. Pike lay on the divan under the window with his boots off and a slice of the cake on his chest. He had not lit the cigar. He had taken it out, instead, and was turning it over in his fingers, looking at it.

“You know what Daddy said about a porch, Texas?”

“I do not.”

“Said a man don’t know who he is till he’s sat on one with nothing in his hands. He said the Army would teach you what you could do, and a porch would tell you what you were.” He rolled the cigar along his thumb. “I think he was lying. I think he just liked a rocker.”

“What does your porch face?”

“The river. Well. A levee. You can’t see the river in Greenville, they built the levee so you couldn’t. You sit there at sundown and the whole Mississippi is on the other side of a hill of dirt, going by, and you can’t see a drop of it. You just know.” He sighed. “Daddy used to say that was the best way to look at most things.”

“It is a reasonable method.”

“And I got the whiskey laid by. And Mama’s rocker. And one cigar, from the Mediterranean.” He put it back between his teeth. The corner of his mouth, where the groove was, twitched. “You’ll come down. After. You’ll sit with me and I’ll light her, and we’ll look at the levee, and I’ll tell you what’s on the other side.”

“I will come.”

“Swear it.”

“I am a Baldwin of Missouri. It is the same as swearing.”

“Good enough.” Pike shut his eyes. “Good enough,” he said again, softer, and was asleep, with the cake on his chest and the cigar unlit, in the lamplight, in the cold gray room of the requisitioned Nancy house, the fourth of December, nineteen hundred and forty-four.

Baldwin turned the page. Through the wall he could hear the typewriters, still going, and from somewhere in the corridor a voice singing, very flat, a Baptist hymn with new words.

The General came in at ten, for his coat. He did not look at the divan. He stopped at the desk, with the new weight on his collar, a little crooked, where he had pinned it himself, and put out his hand, and Baldwin stood and shook it.

“You will draw up the schedule for the Christmas ration,” said the General, “by Wednesday.”

“Yes, sir.”

“I am told there is to be turkey.” He turned in the door. “I am told it is to be a very large quantity of turkey, Baldwin. Do you know I have never in my life seen a turkey that did not seem to me to be a mistake?”
