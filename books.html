The House of Davis

How One St. Louis Family Changed the World

From the banks of the Mississippi

Read Chapter 1 free About the book

The story

It begins at a dry-goods counter on Market Street in 1835 and ends in courts, museums and archives on several continents.

A young merchant from Massachusetts sets up shop on the St. Louis levee. His fortune moves west across the city, to cast-iron Washington Avenue and then behind the gates of Westmoreland Place. One grandson, Dwight F. Davis, puts up a silver bowl in 1900 that becomes the Davis Cup, now played by nations around the world. He builds public tennis courts in Forest Park, serves as Secretary of War and Governor-General of the Philippines, and speaks at the laying of the Wright brothers’ memorial cornerstone at Kitty Hawk in 1928. His brother Samuel builds a private indoor court on Barnes Road for his daughter and leaves a collection of Chinese ceramics to the city's art museum.

Every scene is drawn from the record: letters, cables, receipts, photographs, newspapers and museum files. Where the record goes quiet, the book says so.

From the book

On Sunday morning, January 13, 1929, readers of the St. Louis Post-Dispatch found a family’s private building written up as news. The headline ran across the page in the paper’s heavy capitals: “$60,000 Indoor Tennis Court Built For Girl Champion’s Winter Training.” The deck below it named the owners and the purpose: “Mr. and Mrs. Samuel C. Davis Complete Ornate Structure for Their Daughter, Alita, Near Country Club Grounds.”

Only a few passages of the surviving microfilm print are legible. The story opened with a picture of a young woman at the end of each outdoor season. Alita Davis, the reporter wrote, “placed her tennis rackets in their presses and cases unwillingly each autumn and watched her tanned arms bleach to whiteness as winter months passed with the wish that tennis might be a sport without season.” She had had few chances to work on her game indoors, and when the weather turned she had only “the clay court beside the summer cottage of her father, Samuel C. Davis, on the Barnes road, adjoining the Country Club grounds.” “Tennis,” the story continued, “is a tradition with the Davis family.” A photograph caption calls her “Miss ALITA DAVIS, indoor tennis champion of St. Louis district.” Another names a “$50,000 indoor tennis court”; the newspaper gave both figures. One subhead reads “Floodlights for Night Play.” Another reads “Private Club Started.” The paragraphs under it cannot be read.

The building stood on the Davis property on Barnes Road, at the edge of the Country Club grounds. The Club’s history describes it as a stucco building and “a popular site for tennis parties.” The Post-Dispatch mentions floodlights for night play. Inside was a single court, built so that a young woman’s game would not have to stop each autumn.

Fifteen years earlier, the city’s 1914 guide to its municipal institutions announced tennis courts in Forest Park, with more “to be constructed in 1914,” and courts in O’Fallon Park and Fairground Park. It said that “Permits for lawn tennis issued free.” O’Fallon Park alone had ten courts. The court was the same rectangle in both places, with the same net. The difference lay in who owned the ground, who issued the permit and who could walk in without an invitation. Neither brother left a document saying he saw the contrast; Dwight belonged to private clubs all his life.

Historical fiction · 1944–1945 Colonel Baldwin!

A captain on Patton’s supply staff keeps the Third Army moving with fuel, trains and nerve, from Northern Ireland in 1944 to the end of 1945.

Enniskillen, County Fermanagh, Northern Ireland — Friday, May 12, 1944

The clock over the reception desk of the Lough Erne Arms said five minutes past one, and Capt. Lewis W. Baldwin Jr. of the Third United States Army had an hour and five minutes to deliver himself, two military policemen, a driver from Hamtramck, Michigan, and a brown leather briefcase to the 2:10 for Belfast and the Larne boat. He was not worried. He had never missed a train in his life.

He had the case between his feet. The chain that was supposed to bind it to his wrist had parted on Wednesday at Omagh, on the door of a Nissen hut, in a manner the sergeant at the 8th Division’s headquarters described as “a hell of a thing, sir, I never seen one do that,” and Baldwin had decided to take it as a sign that the Army, like God, was testing him and was not above a joke. The case was ordinary. That was the idea. It was the kind a solicitor might carry into the county court or a cattle buyer to the fair, and it held, in three manila folders, the movement and loading tables for every Third Army unit in Northern Ireland: which regiment would go to which port on which day, in which ship, and in what order its guns and trucks and gasoline would go down into the holds. The folders were stamped TOP SECRET and, beneath that, in a smaller and more cheerful stamp, ORANGE.

Someone at Peover Hall had chosen the word. Baldwin hoped it was a man with a sense of humor, because he was in County Fermanagh, where the Orange lodges marched every July behind fifes and drums and banners of King William on a white horse, and the joke, if it was one, would be understood by everyone in the room except the Germans. He had found it very funny for about a day. Now he found it heavy.

The lobby was the kind he had grown up in, give or take an ocean: dark wood, a carpet that had once been red, brass that somebody polished every morning out of moral duty, and a long window on a street where a gray Irish light made everything look like a photograph of itself. A turf fire burned in the grate and smelled like a farm in heaven. Two RAF officers from the flying-boat base up the lough at Castle Archdale shared a copy of the Impartial Reporter, one reading the front and one the back, and quarreled about a dog. A cattle dealer in a tweed cap told a story to a man in a long black coat, and every so often the dealer laughed, a big wet countryman’s laugh that went up the stairs and came back down. The man in the long black coat did not laugh. He sat very straight, holding a newspaper of his own, and from where Baldwin sat it looked to be upside down.

Baldwin noted it. He noted a great many things. Then he decided that a man holding a newspaper upside down in a hotel in Enniskillen was either a German spy of unusual incompetence or an old fellow who had left his spectacles at home, that the odds favored the spectacles, and he put the thought aside and lit a cigarette.

Historical fiction · 1944–1945 Red Ball

The drivers of one truck company on the Red Ball Express run gasoline and shells across France around the clock, and then try to find their way home.

Utah Beach dumps to the road past Alençon. Friday, August 25, 1944

At dusk on the twenty-fifth of August, First Lieutenant Arthur Pettibone climbed onto the hood of a jeep in an apple orchard behind Utah Beach and called the men of his truck company out from between their trucks so they could see him. It was the first time since he put on the uniform that any officer had wanted Isaiah Cole to see him.

The orchard lay a mile inland from the sand, and somebody’s great-grandfather had planted it in rows so straight you could sight a rifle down them. The apples were small and hard and green. The men had been eating them for a week and paying for it. Forty-eight trucks stood under the trees with their noses pointed out, the way First Sergeant Amos Pruitt liked them, so that no one would ever have to back up in a hurry, and the drivers came out of the dusk between the fenders with mess tins and cigarettes and stood in the grass in a loose half-circle, the way men stand when they do not yet know whether the news is good or bad.

“Gentlemen,” Pettibone said.

A few of them looked behind to see whom he meant.

Pettibone saw them do it. He was a thin man with spectacles and a sunburned neck, and before the Army he had taught algebra to fourteen-year-olds in Muncie, Indiana, forty miles across the line from the Ohio farm he was born on, and he still stood like a man waiting for the back row to settle. He waited now. Then he went on, and he did not take the word back.

“Starting tonight,” he said, “you are on the Red Ball.”

Nobody knew what that was. He told them. On the railroads, he said, a red ball was the hottest freight on the line: the cars that went through ahead of everything, the ones every switchman on the division cleared the track for. The Germans had torn up the French railroads, and so had our own bombers, and the armies had gone running east toward Paris faster than anyone in London had planned for. There was gasoline on the beaches, more than any man in the orchard would ever see again, and there were tanks two hundred miles away standing still for lack of it. The only way to carry the one to the other was trucks. So the Army had taken two roads across Normandy and given them to the trucks, one road out, loaded, and one road back, empty, a loop that would run all day and all night, with nothing else allowed on it.

He had a paper in his hand. He looked at it, folded it, and put it in his shirt pocket as though he had decided it would only make things worse.

“How far, sir?” It was Earl Dabney of Lynchburg, who asked every question twice. “How far, sir?”

“As far as the front is tonight.” Pettibone took off his spectacles and wiped them on his shirt, which made them dirtier. “Farther tomorrow.”
