Chapter 3: Receivership

By John W. Davis. About 3,100 words, roughly 12 minutes.

Peover Hall, Cheshire — May 13, 1944; St. Louis, Texas and Louisiana, 1933–January 1944

Maj. Harlan Pike had been in England seven weeks and had not yet lit the cigar. It was the same cigar, as far as anyone could tell, a long dark Havana he claimed to have taken off an Italian colonel in Palermo, and he carried it in the corner of his mouth the way other men carry a toothpick, moving it from one side to the other when he was thinking, which was always. He was a tall, loose-boned man from Greenville, Mississippi, with a long face, one eyebrow higher than the other, and a voice that took its time. He had come over in March with Col. Muller and the rest of the Sicily men, and he called Baldwin “Texas,” for reasons that went back to the afternoon they met and that Baldwin had long since given up contesting.

Now Pike leaned on the corner of the desk and looked at the message form sticking out of Baldwin’s breast pocket. He could not read it from there. Baldwin was fairly sure he could not read it from there.

“Nice trip,” Baldwin said. “The Irish are charming. They have a great deal of hawthorn.”

“Hawthorn.” Pike moved the cigar. “We had that in Mississippi. We burned it. And the tables?”

“Signed back in.”

“All of ’em?”

“All of them.”

“Well, that’s fine.” Pike went on looking at him, pleasantly, with no expression at all, the way Baldwin imagined he had looked at Italian colonels before he took their cigars. “You want to tell me why the boys in the stable block have got your name on a piece of paper?”

Baldwin looked up.

“I walk around. It’s a failing. My mother said I’d come to no good, and she was right, here I am in England.” He straightened off the desk. “Don’t tell me, Texas. I mean that. I’m only letting you know that I know there’s something to tell.”

He went away down the room toward his own desk, slowly, the cigar traveling from side to side.

Baldwin sat a while with a sheet of paper and Dear Father on it, while the hall clock struck the quarter and a rook in the cedar said something unkind to another rook. He could not write to his father about it. But he found he wanted to, very much, and it surprised him, because he could not remember ever having wanted to tell his father about a mistake.

* * *

The Missouri Pacific went into the hands of a trustee in the spring of 1933, when he was fifteen.

He did not understand it then, or understood it the way boys understand things, as weather. There were men at the house at night, and voices behind the library door, his father’s, which never rose, and other voices, which did. His mother, who was a Dodge and believed that nothing happening in a library could be worse than a draft in the dining room, went on giving dinners. And in the evenings his father sat at the end of the dining table after the plates were cleared, with the ledgers.

He did it every night that winter. Baldwin came home from Lawrenceville for Christmas and found the green-backed books spread over the white cloth where the turkey had been, a pencil in his father’s hand, adding columns he had men downtown paid to add for him.

“Sit down,” his father said, the second night, without looking up.

Baldwin sat.

“Do you know what this is?”

“The ledgers, sir.”

“Which ledgers?”

He did not know. His father turned one of the books around and pushed it across the cloth. It was a list of stations, little towns in Arkansas and Missouri and Louisiana and Texas, Kensett and Bald Knob and Hoxie and Poplar Bluff, and against each there were numbers in a clerk’s beautiful hand: carloads, tonnage, revenue. The numbers were going down steadily, the way water goes down in a tub with a slow leak, every week, every station.

“People think a railroad dies in a wreck,” his father said. “Or a strike, or in the newspapers, or in a courtroom in New York.” He took the cigar from the ashtray. “A railroad dies in the ledgers first. Years first. Those are the men who know.” He tapped the column of stations. “Station agents. Dispatchers. Some fellow at Hoxie who sees four cars of cotton go out where there used to be twelve. He knows the railroad is dying before I do. Before the board does. God knows before the bankers.” He put the cigar back in his mouth. “Remember that. Whatever you do. The man who knows where the thing is, is the man who knows what’s going to happen to it.”

Then he went back to his columns, and Baldwin sat another hour, unable to think how to get up.

It was the longest speech his father ever made him. He went back to Lawrenceville in January with a timetable in his desk drawer where other boys kept Jean Harlow.

His father stayed with the railroad. That was a kind of victory, though it did not look like one, and his mother went on not having drafts. But something had gone out of his father that winter. Baldwin saw it at the end of the table: a smaller man with a bigger cigar.

* * *

The summer after his sophomore year at Princeton he rode the cab of a Missouri Pacific freight from St. Louis to Little Rock and back.

It took arranging. It took his father’s signature, which his father gave without comment. The engineer was a big silent man from Poplar Bluff named Tisdale who chewed tobacco and spat out the cab window with a precision Baldwin found almost musical, and the fireman was a boy not much older than Baldwin who shoveled coal for three hundred miles in a heat that made the cab like the inside of a mouth. Neither spoke to him for the first hundred and twenty miles. He had expected that. He had brought cigarettes.

At De Soto, Missouri, Tisdale took one. At Poplar Bluff, in the yard, he took three, and spat, and spoke.

“Your daddy is a fine man.”

“Thank you.”

“Fine man. Fair man. Never fired an engine in his life.” Tisdale spat. “Don’t know where a train is. Never has. Don’t need to. He’s got the dispatcher to know that for him.”

Baldwin asked what the dispatcher knew.

Tisdale looked at him some time. Then he got down from the cab, and Baldwin followed him across the dark yard and up a flight of wooden steps to a second-floor room with the windows open, a single bulb, and a long table covered with sheets of paper ruled in a grid. At the table a thin bald man in sleeve garters talked into a telephone and drew lines with a pencil, slanting lines, crossing lines, and every line was a train.

“That’s the dispatcher. He knows where everything on this division is, right this minute. Every train. Every car. He knows that passenger’s an hour fifty late out of Hoxie and he knows why. He knows there’s a hotbox on a stock car at Neelyville and a section gang at Qulin don’t want to get up. He knows where every engine is, how much water it’s got. Your daddy knows what’s in the books. This fella knows what’s on the rails.” He spat into the dark. “One day you’re gonna run something, boy. Everybody knows that. Rich man’s son. You remember this fella. You find out who he is wherever you go, and you buy him cigarettes.”

He remembered. He found out who the dispatcher was in every place he went. At Princeton it was the woman who ran the registrar’s office. At Fort Sill it was a mess sergeant. In Cheshire he was working on it.

* * *

He met Anne Fisse at a dance at the St. Louis Country Club in the summer of 1939, in his first week out of Princeton, and he said the wrong thing to her, which was the right thing.

She stood by the French doors onto the terrace in a dress the color of the inside of a shell, not dancing, holding a glass of something with fruit in it and regarding the dancers with scientific interest, as though a university had sent her to study them. She held herself very straight, and the room seemed to tilt toward her a little, and three young men in white dinner jackets had already gone over to ask her to dance and come back. Baldwin had watched it happen. He was a careful watcher of things that did not work.

He went over. He did not ask her to dance.

“I’ve been watching you turn men down. You’ve done three. You do it very well. I’d like to know how it’s done, in case I have to do it in the Army.”

She looked at him. She had brown eyes with a great deal going on in them.

“You’re Warrington Baldwin. Your father runs the railroad.”

“A railroad. There are several. They don’t let one man have all of them.”

She looked a moment more. “I turn them down by looking at them as though they’ve just told me the price of something. It works on men from St. Louis. I don’t know how it would work on the Army.”

“I don’t think anything works on the Army.”

“Then why are you going into it?”

“Princeton made me. I’m a second lieutenant of Field Artillery in the Reserve, which means that if there’s a war I’m the first person they call, and if there isn’t I’m the last person anyone invites to a party.”

She laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, a quick hard surprised laugh like a hiccup, and she put her hand over her mouth, and above the hand her eyes went bright, and he understood that he was going to marry her.

It took a year and a half. She was quicker than he was, and funnier, and a better writer, and she called him Warrington, all three syllables, as his mother did, and in her mouth it sounded like something she had decided to keep. They were married at St. Peter’s on Lindell on Monday, February 24, 1941, in a cold that made the guests’ breath show on the church steps, and afterward his father shook his hand and said, “Well,” and that was one of the other sentences.

* * *

He was called up the next year, learned at Fort Sill to be an artilleryman, turned out to be a good one, and was made a supply officer, because that was how the Army thought.

During training he checked a truck’s tires and marked it ready. The spare was flat. He found this out on a road with a load behind him and a corporal looking at the flat tire, the flat spare and the lieutenant, in that order.

“I’ve left you a choice,” Baldwin said.

“Yes, sir.”

They were there until dark. The corporal showed him how the tools went back into the roll, each in its own pocket, so a man could find one with his fingers. Baldwin wanted to help and mostly held things. When he got back somebody had put his name down for an inspection he had missed.

He remade the bed they had pulled apart, cleaned the mud off the floor and went out to check the truck. Tires, the spare, the fuel, the tools. At the end he rolled the tools up and tied the tapes. The corporal had gone to supper. Nobody watched him do it.

By the end of training the men could hear a loose load before they saw it. They had learned which man’s silence meant he was sick and which man’s meant he was asleep with his eyes open. At breakfast they drank coffee with a skin on it and passed the bread without having to be asked.

The baby came in December 1942, a girl. They named her Anne, and she became “little Anne” at once and then, to her father, Nan. He saw her first on a three-day pass, a small furious red person in a white blanket in his mother’s parlor on Westmoreland Place, and she looked up at him with her mother’s scientific interest and was sick on his uniform.

The Third Army in 1943 was a school with a flag on it, at Fort Sam Houston, and Baldwin came to it as an assistant in the G-4 Section, whom the officers of G-3 called “the grocers.” He found its dispatcher in three weeks, a corporal named Ruiz who walked the headquarters commandant’s Labrador past every office twice a day. At the Louisiana maneuver that August an ammunition train broke down on the main line with a hot journal, with six trains of a division stopped behind it in the heat, and Baldwin, an observer with no authority and nothing to do, walked back to a tin-roofed station that said MISSOURI PACIFIC LINES and asked the agent, a man of sixty with a fly swatter, for the siding that would not be on the Army’s map. There was one, an old lumber spur. When the agent heard his name he said, “Son, you can use my telephone,” and the division arrived eleven hours late instead of two days late.

“Who told you to ask the station agent, Captain?”

“My father, sir. A long time ago.”

After that, in G-4, they called him “the railroad man.”

* * *

The alert came at the New Year’s Eve party at the officers’ club, a little after midnight, on the porch, from a tired G-4 colonel from Fort Worth. “Overseas movement,” he said. “Where do you think?”

“England.”

The colonel put his name in for the advance party and told him to go see his wife.

* * *

He had four days.

He spent the first on a train, the second and third in St. Louis, and the fourth on a train again.

Anne’s face when he told her. She did not cry. She looked at him steadily, and said, “Well, I hope they’ve got decent tea,” and went upstairs, and was gone twenty minutes, and came down with her face washed and her hair done and said, “I’ve told Cook we’re having the lamb.”

His father in the library on the second night, the two of them alone with brandy. His father suddenly very old, older than his sixty-eight years, in the leather chair by the fire with a cigar he wasn’t smoking and the railroad’s annual report unopened by his elbow. They talked about the troop trains, running night and day now, and his father said, “All that traffic, and we can’t keep a single dollar of it,” and smiled. Then a silence. Then his father said, looking at the fire, “Where?”

“They haven’t told us.”

“England.”

“Yes, sir. I think so.”

His father nodded and turned the cigar in his fingers. “You’ll be in supply.”

“Yes, sir.”

“That’s right.” He looked at his son. “That’s where wars are lost. Nobody knows it. Nobody ever writes it down. They lose them in the ledgers first.” He put the cigar in his mouth, finally, and lit it, and the smoke went up between them. “You know who to find.”

“The dispatcher.”

“The dispatcher,” his father said. It was the only time in his life Baldwin heard him laugh at his own joke, a short dry sound, like a book closing.

Then he took out his watch. It was the gold one, a railroad-grade Hamilton he had carried since his Illinois Central days, before Baldwin was born, and it had been set every morning of Baldwin’s life against the clock in the hall. He unhooked the chain from his waistcoat and held it out across the fire without a word.

“Father, I can’t.”

“It keeps better time than the Army.” His father put it in his hand. “Set it by the dispatcher.”

The case was warm from the waistcoat. Baldwin shut his hand around it. Upstairs Nan was asleep in the room where he had slept as a boy. He would have to get the watch home.

And the station, on the last morning. Union Station, its vaulted hall and its thousand men in uniform under the arches, and Anne in her good coat with her hands in a muff, not crying, saying, “Write to me every day, and make it funny, or I won’t read it,” and he said, “I’ll make it hilarious,” and she said, “You’d better,” and he kissed her, and she held his lapels with both hands a moment, very hard, as though she meant to keep them, and let go.

He got on the train. He found a seat by the window and lit a cigarette and looked out at her on the platform, small and straight in her good coat with the steam going past her, and she raised one hand from the muff and did not wave it, only held it up, the way you hold up a hand to say wait, and the train began to move.

* * *

At Peover, under the cedar, Baldwin tore the page with Dear Father carefully off the pad, folded it into a small square and put it in his pocket. Then he took a fresh sheet and wrote:

Dear Anne,

Ireland is green and wet and full of hawthorn. I have brought back no Irish whiskey, which will disappoint Father more than anything else I am allowed to tell you.

He could hear her asking what he was not allowed to tell her. He would have to let her ask. He did not write that she should not worry.

He went on writing. Down the room Pike was on the telephone to someone at a depot, in a slow reasonable voice, saying that if those trucks were not in Knutsford by Tuesday he would personally come down there and explain the situation in Italian. The rook had gone. The cedar was very still.
