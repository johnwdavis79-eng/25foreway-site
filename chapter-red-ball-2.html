Chapter 1: Dub

By John W. Davis. About 3,300 words, roughly 13 minutes.

The truck park at Trappes, France, November 19 to 30, 1944

Staff Sergeant Walter Cole came into the truck park at Trappes on the afternoon of the nineteenth of November on the tailgate of a mail truck, with a duffel bag, a pair of binoculars he had not been asked to turn in, and a flat packet in oilskin inside his shirt, and he stood in the mud for some time looking at what he had volunteered for.

It was a field of trucks. They stood in rows on the old railroad yard behind the station, hundreds of them, deuce-and-a-halfs and ten-ton semis and a few long gray tankers, and every one was the color of the ground. A man could have walked the rows for an hour and not found the end. After the beach, where everything had moved, he could not get used to so many of them standing still.

“You Cole?” said a soldier with a clipboard.

“I’m a Cole. Which one you want?”

“The one that’s a staff sergeant.” The soldier looked him up and down. “You’re supposed to be a boat man.”

“I drove a DUKW.”

“What’s that?”

“A truck that swims,” said Dub, “when it feels like it,” and the soldier wrote that down like a serial number.

The company’s orderly room was a freight office with half a window. In it were a stove, a typewriter, a table with a coffee can of pencils, and a first sergeant standing at the table with his hands behind his back. Dub had seen the first sergeant once before, at a distance, from the sea wall at Omaha on the night of the sixteenth, a straight-backed figure in the lead jeep with the company's lights coming down the draw behind him. Close to, he was taller, and older, and the mustache was whiter, and the left hand behind his back had three fingers, which Dub had been told about and which still surprised him when the hand came out and took his transfer papers.

“Sergeant Cole.”

“First Sergeant.”

“You drive a boat.”

“A DUKW, First Sergeant. It floats. Most of the time.”

Amos Pruitt did not smile. He read the papers through, all three sheets, moving his lips very slightly at the signatures, and laid them down square on the table. “This company has no boats. It has forty-seven trucks, and they do not float, and they do not forgive. You are a staff sergeant, which means that you outrank every man in this company except me and the lieutenant, and that includes your brother.”

“I know that, First Sergeant.”

“I am glad you know it. I would like to be quite certain that your brother knows it. And I would like to be quite certain that he never has occasion to be reminded of it in the hearing of the company.”

“Yes, First Sergeant.”

“Peabody has the motor section. He is a corporal, he is the best mechanic in the company, and he has been asking for a sergeant since October. Go and be a sergeant for him.” He turned to go back to his papers. Then, without turning back: “Sergeant Cole. Your brother has been waiting at the end of the third row since two o’clock. He has said he is inspecting a tire.”

Dub found him there.

He was not inspecting a tire. He was sitting on a drum with the notebook on his knee, writing something down, and he did not look up until the shadow fell across the page. Then he looked up and shut the book and stood, and for an instant, in the gray light with the mud on his boots and his cap pulled low, he looked to Dub like a stranger who had borrowed Ike’s face. It was the stoop. He had always been a big boy and had been inclined to stoop; now he stooped like a man carrying something, though his hands were empty.

“You’re late,” Ike said.

“I’m on time.”

“You said two.”

“I said around two.” Dub dropped the duffel. “What’s the matter with the tire?”

“Nothing’s the matter with the tire.”

“Then you are the worst liar in the Army of the United States,” Dub said, “and I’m glad to see it. I thought they’d changed you.”

The Red Ball had ended on the sixteenth, and the work had not. The trucks that had run it went on running east, in shorter hauls, in the dark, with nobody calling it anything. It was the same work with the name gone.

❦

He was given a cot in the third tent along. Pruitt had put it there. It was the cot at the end of the row nearest the stove, between Ike’s and the tent pole, and it had a coat hook above it and a wooden crate at the head, and on the crate was a Bible with no name on it, because a man with a Bible kept it in his pocket and this one had been left.

“That was Sugar’s,” said Deacon Hollis from the next cot over. He was lying on his back with his boots on and his hands folded across his chest, and he said it as plainly as he said everything. “The bed. The Bible is mine. I’ll move it.”

“Leave it,” Dub said.

“I only left it there because it was a good crate.”

“It’s a good crate.” He sat down. The springs complained. He had heard about Sugar Lyle on the sand at Omaha, over coffee, in Ike’s flat voice, and it had been like listening to a ledger being read aloud. He took his coat off and hung it on the hook, which was empty. It looked like a coat on a hook. After a moment he took the cap off and hung that too.

“You can do that,” said Deacon, though Dub had not asked. “He wouldn’t mind. He’d say a hook is for hanging.”

“Did he say that?”

“He said it about everything. Most of it was wrong.”

Dub laughed, and it caught him somewhere under the ribs on the way out; and he lay back on the cot with his hands behind his head and looked at the canvas, and heard the stove tick, and the men coming in out of the cold, one by one, and the quiet courtesy of the way they did not stare. A man named Greer, from Memphis, nodded to him and said “Sergeant.” A plump man, who must be Tate, shook his hand and said that he had heard a good deal and could prove none of it. A thin careful man with a pencil behind his ear and a face like a ledger said that his name was Banks, that he was from Tulsa, and that he had been told Dub could drive anything and would like to know, for the record, whether this was true.

“I can drive a boat,” said Dub.

“That’s a different thing.”

“It’s a harder thing. A boat is trying to go somewhere else.”

“Make a note,” said Greer.

“I’ve made it,” said Banks, and did.

❦

The next morning he went to work for Peabody.

Corporal Ezekiel Peabody was a small, bald, dry man of thirty who had worked in the Santa Fe shops at Temple, Texas, before the war. He had gone to the maintenance tent in September, after he put his own truck in a ditch asleep, which he regarded as the Army’s way of discovering a talent; and he had, as far as Dub could see, a private understanding with every internal combustion engine in France. He never raised his voice or waved his arms. He laid a hand on the fender of a truck as if on the flank of a horse, and listened, and said, “Number three,” and that was the whole diagnosis, and when they opened the engine it was number three. He had a crew of five and a pile of parts that had been robbed from other parts.

“You can start with the tires,” said Peabody.

“I’m a staff sergeant.”

“That’s what I mean. Staff sergeants do the tires. It’s the only place the Army’s ever found for them.” He handed over a patch kit. “There’s four hundred. We’re two hundred behind. If you find one with no cut in it, bring it to me, and I will frame it.”

He spent a week on his knees in the mud with a tire iron and a bucket. He had thought he knew something about trucks. He had driven a GMC with six wheels and a boat’s keel, had run it up a beach under fire and rebuilt its carburetor with a nail file and the lid of a ration tin; but there was a gap between what a man could do in an emergency and what the road did to a truck over ten thousand miles, and he was learning it through the palms of his hands. The tires were scarred with the Red Ball’s entire geography. He could read them, after a few days: the bruise of the Alençon cobbles, the slash from the broken shell casing in the road below Domfront, the long hard-wear stripe of a driver who rode his brakes. On the fourth morning he worked a bright crescent of tin out of a casing and held it up.

“Shell?” he asked.

“C ration,” said Peabody.

Dub turned it over. There was dried gravy on it.

“They issue us anything that don't come back to the shop?”

“The gravy. Put that in the bucket.”

On the fifth afternoon he painted the sign.

He did it on the back of a ration crate in the lee of a deuce-and-a-half, with a can of white paint that Peabody had been saving for something else. He did it with a rag on a stick, the letters four inches high and not quite straight, and he sat back on his heels and looked at them. DUB’S. He thought a moment. Then, under it, smaller, in a hand that tilted: COLE AND COLE. GARAGE. And under that, because he could not help himself, and because he had once been the kind of boy who could not pass a wall without writing on it: NO JOB TOO SMALL, NO ROAD TOO LONG.

He propped it against the front tire of the nearest truck. It looked, he thought, very fine. It looked like a place where a man could go, in a town, in daylight, with a problem, and be told it could be fixed.

“Sergeant Cole,” said Pruitt, behind him.

He had not heard the first sergeant come across the mud. The man could do that. It was said in the company that Pruitt had learned to walk silently in the Meuse-Argonne, carrying a stretcher past a German outpost, and had never unlearned it, and that the men who had once been startled by it spent the rest of the war walking more quietly themselves.

“First Sergeant.”

“May I inquire what this is?”

“It’s a sign.”

“I can see that it is a sign. It appears to be an advertisement. It appears to be an advertisement for a garage, which this is not, and for an enterprise of which I have no record. May I also inquire who is Cole and Cole?”

“That’s me and Ike.”

“Your brother has not been consulted.”

“He’s going to be,” said Dub. “When we get home. After the war. We’re going to have a garage in Kansas City, Kansas, and he’s going to keep the books, and I’m going to fix the trucks, and between us we’re going to be the only two colored men in the state with a sign over the door that has our own name on it.” He had not meant to say all that. It had come out in a rush, like water from a burst pipe, and he stood there with the rag in his hand and his face getting warm. “It’s not for the Army. It’s for after.”

Pruitt looked at the sign for a long time. The wind turned the corner of the crate. His hand lay quietly at his side, and he did not move it.

“There is a regulation,” he said at last, “against painting on Government property.”

“Yes, First Sergeant.”

“A ration crate is, I believe, Government property.”

“Yes, First Sergeant.”

“I would be obliged to you if you would remove it from view of the lieutenant and the Inspector General’s office by sundown.” A pause. “I would not myself object to its being fixed inside a hood, say, where a man could see it only when he was working on an engine. Where he would need it.”

He turned and went. At the end of the row he stopped, and without looking round he said, “Cole and Cole is a good name for a garage. It says the same thing twice. It is easy to say in the dark.”

“Thank you, First Sergeant.”

“Do not thank me. I am merely noting that you have the proportions right.”

Dub nailed the sign inside the hood of the third truck from the end, and shut the hood on it, and every time one of the company’s men raised that hood in the weeks to follow, they found it there. None of them said a word. It became, after a time, a thing they looked for, as soldiers look for a name carved in a barracks beam; and one of them, Greer, added a line in pencil underneath, saying that the proprietors were gentlemen of color and the parking was free; and after that nobody touched the sign but the company.

❦

On Thursday the twenty-third there was a turkey.

It came by the Army’s decree, in a truck from Paris, in forty-two crates, each with a stenciled paper that said the President of the United States wished all troops overseas a happy Thanksgiving; and the cooks, who had been fed on K-rations for a week and a half, pounced on them like men finding gold. They cooked for eighteen hours. By four in the afternoon the whole freight yard smelled of sage and fat, and the white company that shared the siding, a quartermaster outfit with a captain and a piano, had lined up in the lee of the water tower with their mess kits in their hands, singing something about a hayloft.

The colored company ate second. It was not announced. It had merely been understood, since Utah Beach, that the cooks of the first line had a great many things to carry over to the first line before the second line could be served, and that by the time the second line was served the turkey had been through a good deal of cutting. A thin slice came to each man. Beside it was a spoon of dressing, a spoon of canned sweet potato, a heel of bread, and a smear of cranberry sauce that looked like something from a surgery.

Dub took his tray and sat on a crate. He had been told, and he had said to himself, with dry astonishment, that it was only turkey. He had had worse. He had once eaten a sandwich on the beach at Licata that had been under a dead man’s elbow. He looked at the slice, which was the size of a playing card, and he cut it in half, and he put one half on Deacon’s tray.

“I have my own,” said Deacon.

“Now you have more.”

“It’s a sin to take a man’s bird.”

“Is it? I thought Thanksgiving was the day you were supposed to take it. I read it somewhere. The Pilgrims, they come over, there’s some Indians, there’s a bird, there’s no seconds.” He leaned toward Greer. “Sing.”

“Sing what?”

“Grace. I’m told you do all the voices.”

Greer put down the fork. He was a lean, slow-spoken man with a long jaw, a guitar, and a gift that he used mostly for other people; and he stood, and took his cap off, and cleared his throat, and began, in the voice of the Reverend Hollis of the hill country of Mississippi, which he had never heard and had learned secondhand from the Reverend’s son: “Oh Lord, we are told that we are about to receive. We hope that this is the case.”

There was a silence. Then Deacon, with great dignity, said, “My father does not say that.”

“He’s thinking it.”

“He’s thinking it,” Deacon agreed.

Ike bent over the notebook on his knee. Dub saw the corner of his mouth move.

“What’s he writing?” Dub said to Banks.

“Company strength. Who’s fed. Who’s not. The turkey tonnage, probably.” Banks took a bite. “He does it at every meal. He calls it keeping the count.”

“Of what?”

“Everything.”

Dub looked at the slice of bird on his own tray, the size of a playing card. He ate it slowly, with great care. It was the best thing he had put in his mouth since Sicily.

❦

The last night of November, a Thursday, the stove in the third tent went out at midnight and no one got up to relight it. They lay under their blankets in the dark in their overcoats and their boots with the cold coming up from the ground through the cots, and for a long time nobody spoke. Then Greer began to hum. It was a slow tune, and the others took it up, one by one, not singing, merely humming it, the way a congregation hums when it has forgotten the words and remembers the music, and Dub, on the cot at the end of the row, with his coat on the hook above his head, closed his eyes and listened.

He thought of Licata. He thought of the carbon in the oilskin inside his shirt, and the last line of it, which he could say by heart and sometimes did, under his breath, to make himself laugh: This soldier’s conduct was in the highest tradition of the service. He thought of the company he had come to, which he had not known, and which was, he saw now, not a company at all in the sense he had understood, with the swagger and the crates of beer and the endless good-natured cursing of the boat men. It did not shout. It had a way of being quiet together.

In the next cot, in the dark, he heard a pencil.

He turned his head. The glow of a small flashlight, hooded by a cupped palm, lay on the page of a book. His brother was writing, with the book on his knee, in a hand that did not shake and did not hurry. It was the page on the right. Dub could not see the words. He could see the length of the list, from its place on the page, and the way the lines were close together at the top, and the way the last name was by itself with a space under it, a clean white space, the way you leave room for a man who has not yet come.

He did not ask. He lay and watched the little circle of light move over the paper, and after a time, with the hum going on around him and the stove cold, he said into the dark, very low, so that only one man could have heard, “How many, Ike?”

The pencil stopped.

“Two,” Ike said. “Faulkner and Sugar. The rest on that page are alive. Odom’s on the other page. He’s in hospital in England with his hands burned.”

“That’s all?”

“For now.”

“For now,” said Dub, and shut his eyes, and the light went out.
