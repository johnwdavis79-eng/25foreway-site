Chapter 1: Red Ball

By John W. Davis. About 3,400 words, roughly 14 minutes.

Utah Beach dumps to the road past Alençon. Friday, August 25, 1944

At dusk on the twenty-fifth of August, First Lieutenant Arthur Pettibone climbed onto the hood of a jeep in an apple orchard behind Utah Beach and called the men of his truck company out from between their trucks so they could see him. It was the first time since he put on the uniform that any officer had wanted Isaiah Cole to see him.

The orchard lay a mile inland from the sand, and somebody’s great-grandfather had planted it in rows so straight you could sight a rifle down them. The apples were small and hard and green. The men had been eating them for a week and paying for it. Forty-eight trucks stood under the trees with their noses pointed out, the way First Sergeant Amos Pruitt liked them, so that no one would ever have to back up in a hurry, and the drivers came out of the dusk between the fenders with mess tins and cigarettes and stood in the grass in a loose half-circle, the way men stand when they do not yet know whether the news is good or bad.

“Gentlemen,” Pettibone said.

A few of them looked behind to see whom he meant.

Pettibone saw them do it. He was a thin man with spectacles and a sunburned neck, and before the Army he had taught algebra to fourteen-year-olds in Muncie, Indiana, forty miles across the line from the Ohio farm he was born on, and he still stood like a man waiting for the back row to settle. He waited now. Then he went on, and he did not take the word back.

“Starting tonight,” he said, “you are on the Red Ball.”

Nobody knew what that was. He told them. On the railroads, he said, a red ball was the hottest freight on the line: the cars that went through ahead of everything, the ones every switchman on the division cleared the track for. The Germans had torn up the French railroads, and so had our own bombers, and the armies had gone running east toward Paris faster than anyone in London had planned for. There was gasoline on the beaches, more than any man in the orchard would ever see again, and there were tanks two hundred miles away standing still for lack of it. The only way to carry the one to the other was trucks. So the Army had taken two roads across Normandy and given them to the trucks, one road out, loaded, and one road back, empty, a loop that would run all day and all night, with nothing else allowed on it.

He had a paper in his hand. He looked at it, folded it, and put it in his shirt pocket as though he had decided it would only make things worse.

“How far, sir?” It was Earl Dabney of Lynchburg, who asked every question twice. “How far, sir?”

“As far as the front is tonight.” Pettibone took off his spectacles and wiped them on his shirt, which made them dirtier. “Farther tomorrow.”

Out on the beach a crane was working under lights, and the sound came over the hedgerow, the cough of the engine and the long squeal of the cable, and behind it the low, unending noise of the dumps, which were the size of towns and kept hours like factories.

“There is a speed limit,” Pettibone said. “Twenty-five miles an hour. Sixty yards between vehicles. No passing. Ten minutes’ halt every hour, at ten minutes to the hour.” He put the spectacles back on. “I’m told.”

Someone laughed. Pettibone looked over the heads of the men at the trees. “We roll at midnight. First Sergeant.”

“Sir,” said Pruitt from the back. He had been standing against an apple tree the whole time with his arms folded, and the left hand, the one with three fingers, tucked up under the right elbow, where he kept it when officers were about. He unfolded his arms. “You heard the lieutenant. Fuel, oil, water, tires. Eat now. You will not want to eat later. Move.”

They moved.

Ike Cole walked back to his truck in the purple light and counted things, as he did when he was not yet ready to think.

She was a GMC deuce-and-a-half, six wheels, six cylinders, two and a half tons on paper, and the Army was about to find out what she would carry off paper. He had painted her name on the front bumper himself in England, in white letters a little under three inches high, with a brush he bought from a man in a Hampshire hardware shop who called him “sir” all through the transaction and would not take a penny over the price: MISS LOTTIE. It was for his mother, who had raised four children in Kansas City, Kansas, on a laundress’s wages and a temper that could be seen from Missouri.

He checked the oil. He checked it a second time, because the stick came up dark and he wanted to be sure it was the light and not the oil. He walked around her once with a hand on her, fenders and tailgate and canvas, and kicked each tire, not because kicking told you anything but because his body wanted to do it. Fourteen tires: the ten that rolled, the spare, and three more the motor pool did not know he had, stacked flat under a tarp at the front of the bed where a sergeant with a clipboard would have to climb in to find them. He had learned in the coal yard that a sergeant with a clipboard almost never climbs in.

Deacon Hollis was already in the passenger seat with a can of peaches in his lap and a Bible in his breast pocket. Deacon was nineteen, the son of a preacher from the hill country of Mississippi, and the only man in the company who could sleep sitting upright in a moving truck and wake quoting Ezekiel. Pressed in that Bible, at the Book of Ruth, was a rose that a girl in Alençon had thrown at him twelve days before, brown now and flat as a fingernail. He would not let anyone touch it.

“Red Ball,” Deacon said. “Sounds like a juke joint.”

“Sounds like work.”

“Everything you say sounds like work, Ike.”

“Everything I say is about work.”

“That’s what I mean.” Deacon got his knife into the can and worked it around. “My daddy says a man who talks only about work has got something he isn’t talking about.”

“Your daddy ever drive a truck?”

“My daddy drove a mule named Habakkuk for nineteen years and never once raised his voice to him.” Deacon held out the can. “You want the first one?”

Ike took the first one with his fingers. It was warm and very sweet and tasted of the tin. He stood by the cab and ate it and looked down the line of trucks under the trees, where men climbed on fenders with water cans and lay under axles with flashlights cupped in their hands, and he counted the flashlights without meaning to. He had counted things all his life without meaning to. It was how he kept the world from sliding.

Behind him Sugar Lyle leaned out of his cab.

Sugar was from the South Side of Chicago, and he claimed that before the war he had driven a getaway car for a man so famous he could not say the name. Nobody believed him. Everybody wanted to. He was small and quick, with a pencil mustache that he kept up with a razor blade and a sliver of mirror wired to his side mirror, and he had a way of hanging out of a truck window with one arm along the door as if the truck were a convertible and France were Michigan Avenue.

“Corporal Cole! They tell me there’s a speed limit. Twenty-five miles an hour.”

“That’s right.”

“They say that with a straight face?”

“They wrote it down, Sugar. That’s how you know it’s a joke.”

Sugar laughed and pulled his head in, and then put it out again. “Ike. What’s the hottest freight on the line?”

“You heard him.”

“I’m asking you. On the Illinois Central, I’m telling you, the hottest freight on the line was the one with my uncle Roosevelt on it and a case of rye, and every bull in the Twelfth Street yard knew it, and nobody cleared the track for him, I’ll tell you that.”

“Then this ain’t that.”

“No,” Sugar said, and something crossed his face that Ike would remember later, when he had reason to. “This ain’t that.”

The men in the orchard knew that they had been loading and unloading in the dumps for a month, that nobody on the sand had asked them how long it took, and that tonight they were being told the road, which was a kind of respect.

❦

They loaded through the night at the dump. The dump was a field the size of a county with a road cut through it and lamps on poles along the road. The jerricans were stacked in walls taller than a man, eight and ten deep, with alleys between them for the trucks, so that driving in was like entering a city built out of gasoline. The smell lay over everything, sweet and flat and sickening, and men who had worked the dumps for a month said you stopped noticing it. Ike had worked them for a month, and he had not.

Five gallons to a can, forty-five pounds full. Men of a service company passed them up hand to hand in two lines in the lamplight. They were Black men, all of them, shirts off, backs shining, and some sang and some had no breath to. Ike stood in the bed of MISS LOTTIE and took the cans and stacked them flat, tight, handles up, the way he had stacked coal sacks at fifteen, until the bed was full to the slats and her springs sat down like a tired woman on a porch. Then a forklift came out of the dark with crates of 105-millimeter shells, because the man with the clipboard said the guns up front were counting rounds.

“Not on the gas, Sergeant,” Ike said. “Not shells on gas.”

“Says here mixed load.” The sergeant tapped the clipboard. “You want to argue with the guns?” The crates went on.

The man with the clipboard was a white sergeant with a cold in his nose. He came around with a flashlight and shone it on the load and the springs and the tires and then on Ike, standing up in the bed in his undershirt with a crate of shells in his arms.

“You boys sure you can drive that?”

Ike had driven a coal truck up the bluffs off the Kaw River in January ice since he was fifteen. He had driven it loaded on grades the county closed to horses, at night, with chains on and no lights because the lights had shorted. He had once driven it backward up the Seventh Street hill because reverse was the only gear that would hold. He considered saying so. He considered what it would cost, and what it would buy, and found the price higher than the goods.

“Yes, Sergeant. We’re sure.”

The sergeant wrote something down. He did not look at the load again. He went on to Sugar’s truck, and Ike heard him ask Sugar the same question, and heard Sugar say, “Sergeant, I have driven heavier than this for men you would not want to meet,” and heard the sergeant laugh, a short honk through his cold, and say, “I bet you have.”

Ike took the little notebook from his breast pocket, the cardboard covers gone soft as cloth, and wrote with a stub of pencil by the light on the pole: 25 Aug. Utah dump. 150 cans, 750 gal. 8 crates 105. Truck number. Time. He wrote it as Mr. Kessler had taught him to write a load, what, where, how much, when. He had written about nine thousand loads of coal that way. If you wrote it, it had happened. If you did not, somebody could tell you later that it had happened some other way.

Deacon was asleep upright in the cab with the peach can between his knees.

❦

The convoy rolled at midnight.

There were no headlights, only the blackout slits: on the truck ahead, two thin lines of light low on the tailgate, like a cat’s eyes under a porch. If you lost them you were alone in France. Pettibone’s jeep led, and Ike’s truck was the fifth, behind Rowe and Banks and Odom and Sugar, and behind him came Faulkner and Peabody and Greer and the rest, forty-three more behind his own, back into the dark, none of which he could see. He could hear them: a long, low, unbroken sound like a river, the sound of a company of trucks in second gear.

They went south through Carentan, where the houses on the main street had no fronts and you could see wallpaper and framed pictures in upstairs rooms open to the night. Then St-Lô. Ike had passed through St-Lô in daylight in July, and he was glad of the dark. There was no town there. There were heaps, with a road bulldozed through them, and under the gasoline smell the thing that all Normandy smelled of that summer, which he would not name even to himself. Past St-Lô the signs began, boards nailed on posts at the crossroads, painted white with a red circle, and when the slits caught one it lit like a ball in a carnival game. RED BALL, they said, and an arrow. Some said it in French too.

He kept his eyes on Sugar’s two small lights the way he had once kept them on his father’s lantern, going ahead of him down the coal-yard alley the year he was nine and allowed to ride along on the night deliveries. His father’s lantern used to swing. Sugar’s did not. It rose and fell a little with the road, and wandered left when Sugar got bored, and came back.

Hedgerows went by like black walls. The air came in the window smelling of cut hay and cordite and cattle nobody had buried. Once, on a long straight past Vire, a horse looked over a gate with a trace of light on its eye and was gone. Deacon woke and said, “And the wheels were full of eyes round about,” and slept again.

At the first halt, ten minutes to the hour, they pulled onto the verge with the engines running, because Pruitt had said a cold engine on a cold night might not start again. The men got down and stood in the road and peed into the hedges and smoked with their hands cupped. Ike walked forward to Sugar’s truck.

“Twenty-five miles an hour,” Sugar said. He was sitting on his running board with his helmet off. “I been watching the needle. You know what twenty-five miles an hour is, Ike? It’s my grandmama’s funeral. She weighed three hundred pounds and they had to bring her out the window.”

“Lieutenant’s out front.”

“Lieutenant drives like a man with eggs in his lap.” Sugar put the helmet back on. “I’m only saying. Two hundred miles of this, at a funeral.”

At the next halt nobody said anything. At the one after, past Domfront, Ike found that he could not remember the last twenty minutes of road. He could remember Sugar’s lights. He could remember nothing else. He unhooked the side curtain and drove with his head half out in the wind, and he thought of Pettibone saying sleep when you can, and thought that the lieutenant was a decent man who had never in his life been as tired as he was going to be.

Somewhere past Alençon, an MP waved them through a crossroads with a flashlight, a white MP in a white helmet and a white band on his arm, standing dead center where four roads met. As MISS LOTTIE went by, he lifted his hand to his helmet and saluted.

Ike nearly put her in the ditch.

“Did you see that?”

“I saw it.” Deacon was awake and upright. “Don’t get used to it.”

“He was saluting the truck.”

“He was saluting the gas.” Deacon thought. “Or the shells. Man might have a brother on the guns.”

“He could have been saluting you.”

“Ike, nobody has ever in my life saluted me. If they start in France, I’m not going home.”

❦

At half past five in the morning, in the last of the dark, the column slowed for a burned-out village.

Ike never learned its name. There had been a sign at the edge, but the sign was shot through, and in the dark it was a white board full of holes. The battle had come through two weeks before, going one way and then the other. The church tower stood with its top knocked off like a broken pencil, and the houses along the street had come down into it, so that the engineers had pushed the rubble to either side and left a lane through the middle the width of one truck and a little. You could smell old smoke, the kind that soaks into stone. On the left the rubble stood head-high. On the right it was lower, and behind it was the dark shape of a wall and a garden with fruit trees.

The two little lights ahead of MISS LOTTIE were Sugar’s. The night was quiet except for engines.

Then it was not.

A sound came out of the sky that every man in Normandy had learned in his spine before he learned it in his head: a single aircraft engine, low, coming down the line of the road from behind. It was not ours. Ike knew it the way he knew his mother’s footsteps on the back stairs by where she stopped on the third one. Ours had a steady sound, the sound of men who were not afraid of anything overhead. This one had a note under it, a beat, like a man breathing through his teeth.

“Ike,” Deacon said.

Ahead, Sugar’s lights went out. It was the right thing to do, and it left Ike looking at nothing.

The engine went over so low he felt it in the steering wheel, and something came down out of the dark ahead of Sugar with a sound like a door slammed in another house, and then a sound like hail on a tin roof, running off up the street. He heard the plane climb and turn somewhere off over the fields, and go away, and not come back.

Ahead of Sugar a truck was burning. It burned quietly at first, a low blue flicker under the canvas at the back like a gas ring turned down. Then the canvas caught and the whole back of the truck went yellow, and he could see it: Odom’s truck, the big Alabama boy’s, the number on the tailgate, and stacked inside under the burning cover the same crates of shells he had over his own head. Somebody was shouting. Somebody was running back down the lane toward them in the light.

Ike looked at the fire. He looked up through the rear window of the cab at the crates. He looked at the narrow lane with rubble on both sides, at Sugar’s truck between him and the fire, and behind him forty-three trucks of gasoline nose to tail in a street one truck wide with no way on God’s earth to turn.

He looked at the lower rubble on the right, and at the dark shape behind it, the garden wall, and saw in the firelight that the wall was down in one place, a gap where the stones had spilled into the lane, about ten feet across, with the ground falling away behind.

He had seen a grade like that a thousand times. Every coal chute in Wyandotte County was a grade like that.

“Deacon,” he said. “Get out and run.”

Deacon did not move. “Where you going?”

Ike put MISS LOTTIE in gear and drove toward the fire.
