Chapter 2: What Hour, Captain?

By John W. Davis. About 2,600 words, roughly 10 minutes.

Northern Ireland, Scotland and Cheshire — May 12–13, 1944

Six miles out of Enniskillen, Cpl. Floyd Lusk opened the courier log and licked his pencil.

Baldwin watched him in the mirror. The book was green, with a War Department stamp, and Lusk held it flat on one knee, making a desk of himself. Since Omagh he had accounted for every stop and every hand the case had passed through. He wrote in capitals. A mistake cost him a great deal of rubbing.

“What hour, Captain?”

The case stood on Baldwin’s knees. He laid his hands across its top.

“For the stop, sir. I got one-oh-five for when we come in.” Lusk licked the indelible pencil again. His tongue was purple. “I need when we left. Then I need, uh. The other part.”

There was a whole afternoon he could put into one harmless entry: one-oh-five to two-forty, stopped for telephone call regarding train cancellation. He could dictate it. Lusk would write it. The book would go to London with an hour of telephone trouble in it, and the corporal would have learned how officers did things.

“Put down the truth,” he said.

Lusk’s pencil stopped.

“Arrived the Lough Erne Arms one-oh-five. The courier made a telephone call regarding the train. The party departed at one-twenty without the case.” He heard Szabo stop breathing in the back. “Loss discovered at one-fifty-six. The party returned at two-nineteen and the case was recovered from the custody of the hotel clerk, Mrs. Corrigan, at two-twenty, intact, and examined by the courier and found complete. Departed two-forty.”

Nobody said anything. Dombrowski drove.

“You got that, Lusk?”

“I got—sir, I got most of it. How do you spell custody?”

Baldwin spelled it.

“Sir,” Szabo said from the back. It was more words than he had spoken since Omagh, and he set them down carefully, as if holding something hot. “Sir, that goes to London. That book. They read it.”

“I know they do.”

“Sir, with respect, that’s crazy. Nobody’d know. The lady’s not gonna say nothing, she’s a nice old lady. We could just put two-forty. Nobody’d ever—”

Baldwin turned around in his seat.

He did it slowly, and Szabo went still, and Lusk stopped writing, and Baldwin looked at the two of them, the two military policemen with their helmets on their knees and their faces gone very young.

“Listen to me. There are going to be a lot of things in this war that you and I can’t help. This is one we can. We write it down the way it happened. If a man in London wants to know about it, he can ask me, and I’ll tell him. That’s the courier’s business. That’s my business. It isn’t yours.” He paused. “Here’s your business. You’re going to forget it.”

“Sir?”

“Not the book. The book’s the book. You. You are never going to tell anybody about this afternoon. Not your girl in Omagh, Lusk, or your girl in Belfast, or your girl in every other town in the British Isles. Not your mother, Szabo. Not the chaplain. Not the fellow next to you in a foxhole on the beach when you think you’re going to die and want something off your chest. You get it off your chest, you go right back and find something else.” He was quite calm, and surprised how calm. The cigarette in the lobby had used up all the shaking he had. “If either of you ever talks about this, I’ll find you. I don’t care where you are. I’ll find you and I’ll kill you.” He let that sit. “And then I’ll be court-martialed. Which is the part I really mind.”

The silence in the car went on past a farm, a church and a man with a scythe.

Then Lusk made the noise. He tried to hold it down and it got out through his nose, and Szabo hit him, gently, and then Szabo was laughing too, a deep helpless rumble like coal going down a chute, and Dombrowski at the wheel was shaking without a sound, the way large men laugh in church. Baldwin turned back to the windshield and allowed himself to smile, once, at the hawthorn.

“Yes, sir,” Lusk said, when he could. “Court-martial. We’d hate that for you.”

“I’d hate it too. I’d lose my seniority.”

“Can’t have that, sir.”

“Write it down, Lusk.”

Lusk wrote it down. It took him eleven miles.

* * *

Four hours and twenty minutes to make the seven o’clock boat. Ninety-odd miles. Baldwin checked the road with Dombrowski, looked at the watch once more and put it away. Behind him Lusk went over his entry. At each bad place in the road he lifted the pencil clear and waited.

Baldwin took out his own green notebook, the one with the cracked spine. He opened a clean page and wrote WHAT WOULD THEY NEED.

That was the first rung. His father had taught him to begin with the other fellow, and his father, he suspected, had learned it from losing money. What did the man need?

First, the man would have to know the case was there.

Mrs. Corrigan had seen it. So might the RAF officers, the cattle dealer, Mr. Maguire. Anyone coming down the stairs or in from the street during the hour. He could account for the people he had seen; the others would remain others.

He wrote a second question. How would the man know what was inside?

There was nothing on the leather. An American officer might be carrying requisitions, love letters, a complaint about the bacon. To choose this case out of all the cases in Northern Ireland, someone would have to be waiting for it.

He put a line under the question.

Time. An hour would do. A small camera would do. A room would do. Mrs. Corrigan had a cupboard under her desk and had set the case down square, as she set down her register. He had believed her about the cupboard. He believed her still. He could think of no useful way of entering that in a column.

Dombrowski eased past a cart. Lusk lifted the pencil. The case pressed against the notebook, and Baldwin shifted the notebook to his right knee.

What could the man learn?

He tried to read the loading tables from the other side. The regiments and the ports; the order of the guns and trucks; how much gasoline would go into a ship before the men followed it. No destination across the Channel. No little cross on a beach. Still, a timetable could tell a railwayman a great deal about a train he had never seen.

A photograph might already be going south over the border. Belcoo, Dublin, a courier’s pocket, a diplomatic bag. He could invent a route all the way to Germany. He had no evidence for its first yard.

He had heard that the Germans were watching Patton. His own tables would show a staff making ready for an army to follow somebody else across the water. He could see the shape; another man, with another piece of paper beside it, might see more.

The pencil rested on the page until Dombrowski said, “Dungannon coming up, sir.”

“Thank you.”

Last question. The cheapest way to know.

He wrote:

The log tells the truth. If they ask, I tell the truth. I do not volunteer.

The last four words took very little space.

He shut the notebook and put it in his breast pocket. Lusk’s pencil scraped behind him. At the foot of the hill the sun came out on Dungannon.

* * *

They reached Larne at nineteen minutes to seven.

The steamer lay against the quay, gray with war paint, gangway down. An officer at its foot consulted his watch while the Humber arrived beside him and rocked to a stop.

“You’ll be the Yank in a hurry.”

“I’m the Yank in a hurry.”

“Well, you’re not in a hurry now. Twenty minutes. I could have had a cup of tea.”

Lusk closed his eyes.

They left the borrowed car with the movement staff and carried their kit aboard. Baldwin went to the upper rail. He held the case in his left hand, with a finger through the handle, and used his right to light a cigarette. The match burned steadily. Ireland lay beyond the quay, blue hills, chimneys, the lough. He had had enough of it to last him.

Lusk arrived on deck with the log under one arm and a cup in his free hand, walking carefully. He had finally got his tea, and he was unable to drink it, because every time he lifted the cup the book began to slide. Szabo came after him with two cups, drank one and took Lusk’s book without asking.

“That’s an official book,” Lusk said.

“I know.” Szabo tucked it under his arm.

“You got tea on your hand.”

“I got tea in me.”

Lusk examined his cup for a lid it didn’t have. The ship gave a small movement against the quay and he stopped arguing. Szabo kept the log against his side while Lusk drank, and returned it dry. When the cups were empty they went below together, Szabo leading the way with both cups in one hand. Lusk tried to tell him something about the book. He was still telling him when the saloon door closed.

Dombrowski came up behind him.

“MPs are settled in the saloon, sir. Szabo’s on his third cup now.”

“And Lusk?”

“Girl from the Wrens. She’s got a fiancé on a battleship. Lusk wants to know which one. Says he’d like to send him a card.”

“How is that going?”

“She’s not telling him, sir.”

A gull landed on the rail and inspected them.

“Sir. The killing part. What you said in the car. Would you have?”

Baldwin looked at him. Dombrowski waited.

“No. I’d have tried to court-martial them. I’d have failed. They’re too stupid to convict.”

The sergeant’s laugh drove the gull off the rail. He reached into his blouse and brought out a flat paper package tied with string.

“Favor, sir. If you don’t mind. I’m afraid the MPs’ll sit on it.”

“What is it?”

“Stockings, sir. Silk. Real silk, not that rayon stuff. Fella in Omagh. Don’t ask me where he got ’em.”

“I won’t.”

“They’re for my mother.”

Baldwin looked at the package, and at Dombrowski.

“Your mother.”

“Yes, sir. She’s a petite woman.” Dombrowski’s face was perfectly grave. “She lives in a bar in Hamtramck.”

Baldwin held it as long as he could, the way he had held the cigarette in the lobby. Then it came out of him, the real laugh, the one his wife said she had married him for because she had never heard a man laugh with his whole chest, and it went out over the water toward Ireland, and two Wrens and a chaplain on the deck below turned to look up.

“Lives in a bar.”

“Owns it, sir. On Joseph Campau. Lives over it. Same thing in Hamtramck. She’s been there since my old man died. She’s four foot eleven. She can throw a man from Dodge Main out the door by his belt. Nobody in Hamtramck’s seen real silk stockings except in the pictures, and I want her to have ’em, and I want her to wear ’em to Mass at St. Florian’s and walk up to Communion so slow every woman in the parish has to look.”

“You’ll want to post them from Knutsford,” Baldwin said, when he could speak. “Give them to me. I’ll keep them in the case.”

He saw Dombrowski hesitate. He saw him look at the case, understood the hesitation, and loved him a little for it.

“Sergeant. Nothing that goes in this case is ever going to leave my hand again until I’m dead. You have my word. Your mother’s stockings are the safest thing in the British Isles.”

Dombrowski gave him the package. He opened the case there on the deck, laid the stockings on top of the ORANGE tables, and closed it. The case weighed what it had always weighed, and he was absurdly glad.

* * *

At Stranraer late that evening they found a compartment to themselves in the waiting train. Blackout blinds, blue bulbs, a stale cushion against Baldwin’s shoulder. Lusk and Szabo settled opposite with their helmets between them. Dombrowski put his head back. He had sung all the way from Omagh that morning; he had found nothing to sing since.

The courier book lay closed in Lusk’s lap, pencil in its loop. Baldwin wanted to check the times. Lusk was already asleep, with his mouth open. Baldwin left him asleep.

The train moved. The case was on Baldwin’s knees. When he woke it was still there.

* * *

They came into Cheshire in a fine soft rain. A truck took them from the station through the lanes and black-and-white houses to Over Peover, and in through the gates of Peover Hall, which the men called “Peever” because the British did and it was easier than arguing. The chimneys stood over wet brick. Somewhere behind the windows the general was beginning his day. Baldwin took the case to the G-4 Section in the old library.

At the first door Lusk halted to find the page where he had written the times, folding a thumb into the book to keep it open. The pencil mark across the side of his hand had survived the crossing. Szabo took the other handle of his kit bag while Lusk read his own entry once more, lips moving. He looked up when Baldwin stopped beside him.

“Custody’s right,” Lusk said.

“You spelled it right.”

“That’s what I was checking, sir.”

The warrant officer who kept the G-4 safe counted the folders against the list and gave Baldwin a receipt. Lusk passed over the log.

The warrant officer read it standing up. At the Enniskillen entry he pulled out his chair and sat.

“Says here you misplaced the case, sir.”

“For about an hour.”

“Recovered intact.”

“Yes.”

He went back to the beginning of the entry. A thick-necked Pennsylvanian, an accountant before the war; Baldwin had watched him return a perfectly good requisition because its carbon had failed to record a comma. His finger went down the times.

“The book goes up the line.”

“I know.”

“Long way up, these days.”

“I know.”

The warrant officer set the log in the OUTGOING basket. The case went into the safe. He turned the key.

In the passage Dombrowski waited with the paper package.

“I’ll post ’em this afternoon, sir.”

“Tell your mother I said to walk slow.”

“Yes, sir.”

Baldwin slept most of Saturday. After supper he went back to his desk in the old library. Through the window the cedar dripped onto the grass. His requisitions waited in the order he had left them on Monday. A folded buff message lay on top.

HQ THIRD US ARMY — G-4 Capt. L. W. Baldwin Jr. Maj. G. Tunney, Field Security, would be grateful for a word with Capt. Baldwin regarding Irish traffic. Tuesday 16th, 1000 hrs. Room 9, the Stable Block.

He read it twice and folded it along the old crease and put it in his breast pocket beside the notebook, against the page that said I do not volunteer.

He sat down and lit a cigarette. Behind him a slow Mississippi voice said, “Well, Texas. You look like a man who’s had a nice trip.”
